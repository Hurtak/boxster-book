# Manual transmission and clutch

A manual car gives the driver two separate controls. The clutch pedal connects the engine to the gearbox or separates them; the gear lever chooses how fast the engine turns for a given road speed. The 2010 Boxster 2.9's standard gearbox has **six forward speeds and reverse**. Porsche calls it the **G87/10**, distinct from the S model's G87/40.[^manual-id]

The engine sits ahead of the gearbox in the rear power unit. Drive passes from the crankshaft through the flywheel and clutch to the gearbox input shaft, through the selected gear pair, then through the final drive to the differential and rear driveshafts. The gearbox and final drive share the transaxle assembly but do different jobs. This chapter covers selecting and interrupting the drive; [Differential, driveshafts and mounts](../10-differential-driveshafts-and-mounts/index.mdx) follows it to the rear wheels.

## The flywheel and clutch make a controllable connection

The clutch lets the engine run while the car stands still. The 2.9 manual uses a **single-plate dry clutch and a dual-mass flywheel**, with a 240 mm pressure plate according to Porsche's technical introduction.[^clutch]

The flywheel is attached to the crankshaft, and its spinning mass carries the engine smoothly between firing pulses. A dual-mass flywheel splits that mass into an engine-side mass and a gearbox-side mass joined through a spring damper. The two can twist a little relative to each other, so the springs reduce how much of the engine's torsional vibration reaches the gearbox. This is not clutch slip: the flywheel remains a drive connection while it damps the variations in it.[^dmf]

The clutch's driven plate has friction linings on both faces and a hub splined to the gearbox input shaft. A spring-loaded pressure plate squeezes it against the flywheel's friction face, and once it is clamped without slipping it turns with the flywheel and carries the engine's torque. The spring does the clamping, so with your foot off the pedal the drive stays connected without effort.[^clutch-principle]

<Figure src={illustration2} alt={"Two clutch sections compare the pedal-up state, with the friction plate clamped, against the pedal-down state, with pressure relieved. A separate path shows pedal force reaching the release mechanism through the master cylinder, fluid and slave cylinder."}>

Pedal up, the spring clamps the friction plate; pedal down, the clamping pressure is relieved. Below, the reservoir feeds the master cylinder, and pedal pressure reaches the release mechanism through a separate slave cylinder.

</Figure>

Pressing the pedal moves the release bearing against the diaphragm spring, letting the pressure plate back off enough to free the driven plate. The flywheel keeps turning while the input shaft slows or turns at another speed; the bearing lets the release force act on the spinning clutch.

Between fully released and fully clamped, the clutch passes torque while its two sides still turn at different speeds. That controlled slip lets you move off without the engine being dragged down to zero rpm, but it makes heat. The heating rate depends on the torque passed multiplied by the difference in rotational speed. Once both sides turn together, that source of friction heat falls away. Prolonged slipping therefore heats the clutch more than a brief, smooth engagement.

"Dry" means the friction surfaces are not bathed in oil. The gearbox has its own lubricating oil, and the clutch release its own hydraulic circuit.

## How the pedal releases the clutch

The clutch pedal pushes a piston in a **master cylinder**, sending fluid through a pipe and hose to a **slave cylinder** on the gearbox. The slave cylinder pushes a pivoted release lever, which moves the release bearing along a guide sleeve around the input shaft that keeps it square to the clutch. Porsche's parts catalogue lists these as separate components for the G87/10, so the diagram shows a slave cylinder and lever rather than an integrated concentric release unit.[^release]

<Figure src={clutchParts} alt="Clutch exploded drawing separating the pressure assembly and driven plate from the release lever, bearing and guide sleeve." width="560px">

The clutch set (1) groups the pressure assembly and single driven plate; the release lever (3) moves the bearing (4) along the guide sleeve (5). The plate is shared with S applications, but that does not make the S's self-adjusting clutch standard on the 2.9. Factory drawing: Porsche AG, [PET 301-000, PDF p. 109](https://www.porsche-preparation.com/downloaded/pet-porsche/boxster-2009-2012-type-987.pdf#page=109).

</Figure>

Hydraulics let the pedal sit far from the clutch without a rigid linkage running down the car. Fluid pressure on a piston produces force, and the cylinder sizes, with the pedal and lever geometry, trade travel against force. The circuit carries only the driver's effort, not the clamping force.

The circuit draws fluid from the brake-fluid reservoir, although clutch and brakes have different actuators and purposes. A feed line supplies the master cylinder and a pressure line carries the pedal's command rearwards. The pedal has its own assist spring, separate from the diaphragm spring. Air, a leak or a sticking part can shorten the release travel. The clutch can then drag with the pedal down, making gears hard to select even when the gearbox is sound.[^hydraulics]

A full release matters during a shift: it unloads the engaged gear and lets the parts for the next ratio change speed. Porsche's handbook asks for full pedal travel and complete gear engagement, and warns against holding the car on a steep slope with a partly pressed clutch. The standard Drive-Off Assistant holds the car briefly on a hill with the brakes; it does not make the manual clutch automatic.[^handbook]

## What the gear lever actually moves

Two cables link the gear lever to the gearbox. Brackets hold their outer sleeves, and their inner ends join the lever to the gearbox selector; Porsche says this keeps drivetrain vibration out of the lever. Moving the lever forwards and back, and across the gate, tells the selector mechanism which gear to engage, and selector forks then slide the matching engagement sleeves.[^lever]

## Constant mesh and synchronisers

In a constant-mesh gearbox the main forward gear pairs are always in mesh. Some gears are fixed to a shaft; their mating gears spin freely on their own shaft until a toothed sleeve locks them in place. The sleeve, not the meshing teeth, chooses which gear drives the shaft.

**Neutral** means no forward or reverse gear connects the input to the final drive, though input-side parts can still turn with the clutch up. **Clutch down with a gear selected** is different: the gear is engaged, but the clutch has cut it off from the engine. Pedal and lever interrupt the drive in separate ways.

<Figure src={illustration4} alt={"A simplified two-ratio constant-mesh gearbox shows both gear pairs meshed, with one free-running wheel locked to the output shaft by a sliding sleeve. A three-stage strip explains cone friction, matching speeds and tooth engagement."}>

A simplified two-speed gearbox: both gear pairs stay in mesh, and a sliding sleeve locks one free-running gear to the output shaft. The strip shows the synchroniser's three stages: cone friction, matched speeds, then tooth engagement.

</Figure>

A **synchroniser** helps make the engagement quiet and manageable. As the selector starts moving a sleeve, a friction cone brings the chosen gear and the shaft's hub towards the same speed, and a blocking action keeps the teeth apart until the speeds match. The sleeve then slides over the gear's dog teeth and locks it to the shaft. Friction matches the speeds; the teeth carry the drive.[^synchroniser]

At a steady road speed, an upshift needs the gearbox input to slow down and a downshift needs it to speed up. The synchroniser makes that change inside the gearbox while the clutch is released; letting the clutch in then brings the engine to the speed the new gear demands.

Reverse adds an extra gear to its path so that the output turns the other way. Its ratio is **3.333:1**, distinct from first's 3.667:1, and the handbook requires the car to be stationary before it is selected. The handbook is the authority on operation; the simplified forward-gear drawing is not a plan of the G87/10's internals.[^ratios][^handbook]

## Ratios exchange speed for torque

A first-gear ratio of **3.667:1** means the input turns about 3.667 times for each turn of that gear's output. The final drive adds a second reduction, and multiplying the two gives the overall reduction between the engine and the differential carrier, with the clutch fully engaged.

Ignoring losses, a reduction multiplies torque by the same factor that it divides speed, much as a lever trades distance for force. For the same engine input, first therefore gives more torque and less speed at the wheels than sixth. The explorer applies this to the 2.9 manual's ratios.

At an illustrative **200 Nm and 3,000 rpm**, first's overall reduction gives about **2,842 Nm** and **211 rpm** at the differential carrier. That torque is the total before it is split between the wheels. Torque rises as speed falls; in this ideal calculation the power stays the same.

The explorer assumes an illustrative **2.10 m rolling circumference**, no clutch or tyre slip, equal rear-wheel speeds and no transmission losses. Wheel speed is engine rpm divided by the overall reduction; road speed is wheel rpm multiplied by the circumference, converted from minutes to hours. The torque shown is the **total after the final drive**, before it is shared between the wheels.

It shows how an upshift at fixed road speed lowers the engine speed, but does not predict acceleration: real engine torque varies with speed and throttle, tyres deflect under load, and the car must overcome inertia and road resistance. ## Options on the 2010 2.9

The main alternative is the seven-speed **PDK**, which operates two wet clutches and selects gears itself; [PDK transmission](../09-pdk-transmission/index.mdx) covers it and its Sport Chrono functions.

On a manual car, Sport Chrono leaves the six ratios and the driver-operated clutch unchanged; its other effects are in the [integration matrix](../18-occupant-protection-and-system-integration/index.mdx#options-on-the-2010-2.9).[^chrono]

A short shifter shortens the lever's travel, not the gearing. Porsche's contemporary **North American Exclusive Boxster** catalogue lists one as **XCZ** for the Boxster and S, and the separate 987 Tequipment catalogue describes the accessory mechanism, noting that the shift can be more restricted when cold. Genuine Porsche short-shift equipment therefore existed, but the January 2010 UK option table for the ordinary car does not list XCZ. Check factory availability for a **2010 UK 2.9** against the car's dated order record; no percentage reduction in throw is given here.[^short]

## Changes in other 2.9 model years

The six-speed G87/10 arrived with the 2009 revision, together with a revised housing and engine flange and a changed fifth ratio, so the earlier base car's five-speed gearing does not apply.[^manual-id] The January 2010 UK catalogue still lists six speeds. The [annual matrix](../19-reference-atlas-and-index/index.mdx#annual-equipment-matrix) separates later US evidence on gearing from UK revisions that remain unresolved.

The parts catalogue records one smaller change: clutch tube item 10 has one listing up to model year 2009 and another from model year 2010. The feed pipes and forward clutch tube also differ between left- and right-hand drive. These are plumbing differences; the catalogue says nothing about a change in pedal feel or clutch capacity.[^later]

## The S, Spyder and Black Edition

The ordinary S also has six manual speeds, but Porsche calls its gearbox the **G87/40** and records further final-drive and sixth-gear changes at the facelift. The training material assigns the **self-adjusting X-Tend clutch to the S models**. It compensates for lining wear inside the pressure assembly, and the source does not describe it as a feature of the basic 2.9's clutch. The parts catalogue also lists separate basic and S flywheels, release bearings and pedal springs.[^clutch][^manual-id]

The Spyder and the Boxster S Black Edition keep a standard six-speed manual and optional PDK behind their higher-output 3.4-litre engines; their catalogue performance reflects their own engines, weights and equipment. "Six-speed" alone does not mean identical clutches or ratios. For the later **UK Black Edition**, the **March 2011** price list offers the **XCZ short shifter** at **£388 including 20% VAT**, with the manual gearbox only.[^special]

## Identification and ageing

The identification on the installed G87 gearbox tells you which transmission is fitted, whatever has been done to the lever or knob.

Worn or contaminated linings, or lost clamping force, can let the clutch slip. Short release travel can make it drag. Worn cables or joints can upset selection before a fork or synchroniser is involved. A dual-mass flywheel can deteriorate in its damping mechanism while the driven plate still has usable lining. It helps to tell apart slip under load, incomplete release, lost lever movement and resistance in one particular gear.[^ageing]
