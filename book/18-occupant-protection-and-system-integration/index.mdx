# Occupant protection and system integration

A Boxster works as a whole because its parts share loads, information and responsibilities. The tyres connect it to the road and the suspension keeps those connections controlled; the engine and transmission supply torque and the brakes remove energy. Around the occupants, the body, seats, belts and airbags form another connected system. This chapter brings the book's mechanisms back together in one car.

The 2010 European/UK 2.9 has an open cabin, but its protection does not rely on the fabric hood acting as a structural roof. It combines a reinforced windscreen frame and permanent rear rollover bars with three-point belts, pretensioners, force limiters and six airbags. The side head airbags are in the **doors**; the thorax airbags are in the **seats**.[^uk-restraints]

## A fixed structure around the cabin

The [body chapter](../04-body-structure-and-packaging/index.mdx#stiffness-and-occupant-protection-have-different-aims) explains how the shell carries loads and where it deforms. Keeping the passenger compartment intact preserves space; the restraints then have to manage the occupants' continuing motion.[^uk-restraints]

Without a fixed roof, two parts of that compartment do the protective work a roof would otherwise share. A steel reinforcement is built into the windscreen frame. Behind the seats stand high-strength steel rollover bars: two hoops and a cross-member forming one roll-bar assembly, separate from its trim covers, with the upper belt anchorages attached. These are permanent structural parts. The 987.2 arrangement shown here has no sensor-triggered extension or deployable rear hoops.[^uk-restraints]

<Figure src={illustration4} alt={"An original schematic showing a reinforced windscreen frame at the front and two fixed steel rollover bars behind the seats, with upper belt anchorage points on the rear bars."}>

</Figure>

The soft top and its bows only keep the weather out; opening or closing the hood neither assembles nor withdraws the steel structure. Because the bars are fixed, the removable wind deflector described in the [roof chapter](../14-roof-glass-seals-and-drainage/index.mdx) can use the space between and around them, carried by a structure that is already there.

## Seat belts

When the car slows, the people inside keep moving. The restraints must bring each occupant down to the car's changing speed while controlling contact with the interior. The belt makes the first connection, across the pelvis and upper body; an airbag adds a broader surface in the directions it is designed for. The seat and its mountings support the occupant and locate them relative to both, which is why [seat adjustment](../15-cabin-seating-and-climate/index.mdx#a-seat-is-a-support-structure) matters. Secure anchorages and clear deployment space complete the arrangement.

The three-point belt's inertia-reel retractor lets the webbing move slowly but locks under sudden deceleration. The shoulder section crosses the upper body and the lap section sits low across the pelvis. The upper guide is carried by the rear-bar assembly, the outer lower anchorage by the body, and the inboard buckle by the seat. Normal reel locking is one function; crash pretensioning and force limiting add two more.[^belt-handbook]

A **pretensioner** removes slack when triggered, so the belt starts restraining earlier. A **force limiter**, or load limiter, then controls belt tension as the load rises. The first reduces free movement before restraint begins; the second controls the force passed through the belt once it has.[^belt-principles]

<Figure src={illustration2} alt={"A schematic occupant with diagonal and lap belt paths connected to the passenger cell. Three boxes distinguish normal reel locking, removal of slack by the pretensioner and control of belt force by the limiter."}>

Reel locking, pretensioning and force limiting work together; none is a setting the occupant chooses. The arrows show how the occupant connects to the anchorages; their lengths do not represent measured forces or belt travel.

</Figure>

Porsche's period handbook describes the pretensioners as single-use and links their fault indication to the airbag warning light. A belt that looks tidy after a collision is only part of the evidence: the webbing, retractors, buckles, anchorages and triggered components all matter.[^belt-handbook]

## Airbags

The ordinary Boxster and S have two front airbags, in the steering-wheel centre and the passenger side of the dashboard. Porsche Side Impact Protection, **POSIP**, adds two on each side: a thorax bag in the outer seat bolster and a head bag in the door, making six in all. The UK brochure gives approximately eight litres for each POSIP bag and states that they protect with the hood open.[^uk-restraints]

Each airbag stays folded in its module until the restraint control unit fires its inflator. Gas fills the bag fast enough to put an extra cushion between the occupant and the interior. It supplements the belt. Because inflation is so rapid, the bag needs clear space, and the occupant's position matters.[^airbag-principles]

Porsche's handbook links front-airbag deployment to a sufficiently severe frontal collision, and side-airbag deployment to a corresponding impact on that side. The decision rests on direction and severity, not a single road-speed threshold. The crash-sensing system runs its own diagnostics, separate from PSM's monitoring of vehicle motion. Porsche's parts drawing lists the airbag control unit, side crash sensors and front sensors separately from the bag modules, with separate wire sets for the seat and door modules and belt tensioners. It identifies the hardware but not the deployment calibration.[^airbag-handbook]

The January 2010 UK brochure describes Porsche Tequipment child-seat preparation: an ISOFIX fixing bracket, carried by the seat frame according to the parts drawing, and a key switch to deactivate the passenger airbag. It excludes sports bucket seats. The North American handbook also describes passenger-seat weight sensing under US regulations; that system is not assigned to the UK reference car.[^child]

## One bend, many systems

Picture a 2010 2.9 braking into a bend. Deceleration shifts load onto the front wheels, and the tyres produce the forces that slow and turn the car. Springs, dampers, bushes and geometry govern how those contact patches are supported, while the driver turns the front wheel carriers through the steering linkage. The [shared grip budget](../12-steering-wheels-and-tyres/index.mdx#slip-and-the-shared-grip-budget) explains why, in a simplified picture, more braking leaves less grip for cornering.

As braking eases and drive is applied, torque reaches the rear tyres through the manual or PDK gearbox, final drive, differential and joints. The differential lets the outside rear wheel travel further than the inside one. If its sensors call for correction, PSM can brake individual wheels and cut engine torque. Each request changes a mechanism's output, but the tyres still have to produce the road forces. The example shows who does what; it does not predict an intervention in any particular bend.

## From request to physical action

Driving control works on a different timescale and for a different purpose from crash protection. Three questions help follow it: **what is measured, which control unit makes the request, and which physical part acts?** Electrical messages carry information; the force still comes from combustion, clutch friction, tyre grip or hydraulic pressure.

Take the accelerator. Its position is a sensor reading of the driver's demand. DME, the engine-management system, turns that demand into engine control, using its other inputs and operating limits, and the throttle, injectors and ignition change the torque. The [engine-control chapter](../06-air-fuel-ignition-and-engine-control/index.mdx#the-accelerator-asks-for-torque) follows the process in the 2.9.[^pedal]

With optional PDK, the transmission control unit and DME cooperate on each shift. The clutches and gear selection change the torque path through the gearbox, while coordinated engine-torque control smooths the handover. With the standard manual, the driver's clutch and lever do the selecting and engaging. Both routes end at the final drive and tyres, but they are controlled differently.[^pdk-control]

PSM can make requests of its own, whichever gearbox is fitted. It monitors wheel speed, steering angle, lateral acceleration and yaw rate, and can correct the car by braking individual wheels and reducing engine torque. So an unchanged accelerator position does not guarantee unchanged torque. The [ABS and PSM explanation](../13-brakes-and-stability-systems/index.mdx#abs-and-psm) covers wheel-pressure control; here the point is that the brake and engine control units cooperate.[^psm-control]

## Options on the 2010 2.9

PSM and the restraints are standard. **Sport Chrono 639** and **Sport Chrono Plus 640** change functions that depend on the other equipment fitted; neither raises the 2.9's rated 188 kW / 255 PS. Plus requires PCM, while PDK and PASM are ordered separately.[^modes]

| Function | Manual + Sport Chrono | PDK + Sport Chrono | Additional equipment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sharper accelerator characteristic and harder limiter response in Sport | Yes | Yes | Engine-control change; [chapter 6](../06-air-fuel-ignition-and-engine-control/index.mdx#options-on-the-2010-2.9) |
| Later PSM intervention threshold in Sport | Yes | Yes | Standard PSM; [chapter 13](../13-brakes-and-stability-systems/index.mdx#options-on-the-2010-2.9) retains OFF/reactivation conditions |
| Performance-focused shift programme | Driver still selects gears | Yes | PDK; [chapter 9](../09-pdk-transmission/index.mdx#options-on-the-2010-2.9) |
| Sport Plus and Launch Control | No | Yes | PDK and Sport Chrono |
| PASM Sport programme request | If PASM fitted | If PASM fitted | Separate PASM 475; its button can select the damping programme independently |
| PCM performance display and personal memory | Plus only | Plus only | PCM and Sport Chrono Plus 640 |

The stopwatch is the visible part of the package, not a guide to every dependency. PASM varies damping; it does not change spring rate or raise and lower the body on demand. The [brake-mode conditions](../13-brakes-and-stability-systems/index.mdx#options-on-the-2010-2.9) separate ordinary vacuum assistance from the additional hydraulic brake assist and explain PSM OFF.[^psm-control]

The UK launch and January 2010 catalogues both document this restraint layout.[^years] Later UK sensor, component or software changes remain marked U, unverified, in the [annual matrix](../19-reference-atlas-and-index/index.mdx#annual-equipment-matrix).

## The S, Spyder and Black Edition

The ordinary S shares the UK brochure's restraint description with the 2.9. The lighter Spyder keeps door side-impact protection, rollover bars and an airbag system; its standard sports bucket seats help save weight. Porsche lists separate side-airbag modules for those seats, so lighter seating does not mean deleted airbags. The Spyder brochure also describes PSM and optional Sport Chrono.[^variants]

The Black Edition's UK specification keeps the front airbags, POSIP, belt pretensioners and force limiters. Its black-finished rollover bars are a visual treatment of the same arrangement. PDK, PASM and Sport Chrono Plus remain options.[^black]

## Identification and ageing

Take care with seat changes, because the original seats contain the thorax airbags. Check the fitted seats, steering wheel and diagnostic condition together. A lit warning lamp reports a fault; an unlit one is neither a structural survey nor a full repair history. Porsche directs airbag faults and restraint work to qualified service personnel.[^airbag-handbook]

The same distinction runs through the whole book. A part name identifies a design; inspection establishes the condition of the car in front of you. A mode changes a control unit's instructions, but tyres, springs, dampers and brakes still produce the forces. Belts and airbags depend on the body and seats that locate them. Together, the Boxster's mechanisms form one chain: from the driver's request, through controlled torque and wheel forces, to the structure and restraints around the people inside.
