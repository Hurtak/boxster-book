# Differential, driveshafts and mounts

The gearbox has one output, but the Boxster has two driven rear wheels, and in a corner they must turn at different speeds. The differential, a set of gears, drives both wheels while letting their speeds differ. A short driveshaft on each side then carries the drive to a wheel that moves with the suspension. Mounts hold the joined engine and gearbox in the car while letting them move a little.

The standard six-speed manual and the optional seven-speed PDK differ inside, but both must drive two independently suspended rear wheels.[^drive-layout]

## From the gearbox to the wheels

The gearbox and final drive form one assembly behind the engine, the **transaxle**. The selected gear sets the ratio between engine speed and gearbox output; the **final drive** adds a second, fixed reduction. Shifting from second to third changes the selected ratio, not the final-drive gears. The manual and PDK chapters cover the earlier steps, and the [ratio explorer](../08-manual-transmission-and-clutch/index.mdx#ratios-exchange-speed-for-torque) calculates the combined reduction.[^drive-layout]

In Porsche's PDK training cutaway, a pinion drives a large ring gear through a **hypoid** gear set, turning the drive across the car and reducing its speed. The ring gear is attached to the differential carrier, which runs in bearings in the casing; output flanges connect it to the two driveshafts.[^final-drive]

## The open differential

Inside a conventional **open differential**, two side gears drive the left and right outputs. Smaller bevel pinions mesh with both and are carried round by the differential carrier. Two movements can therefore happen at once: the carrier spins with the ring gear, and the pinions can also turn on their own cross-axis.[^final-drive]

On a straight road, with equal tyres, both wheels turn at the same speed and the pinions stay still on their axis. In a bend the outside wheel travels farther, so the pinions turn, letting one side gear run ahead of the carrier while the other drops back by the same amount. For a symmetrical differential:

**Carrier speed = (left output speed + right output speed) ÷ 2.**

A carrier at 300 rpm can drive the wheels at 240 and 360 rpm, or equally at 0 and 600 rpm, which is how one wheel can spin while the other stands still. The equation cannot say which wheel will spin; grip, load, braking and inertia decide that.

An ideal open differential also sends **equal torque** to both outputs, whatever their speeds. Power is torque multiplied by rotational speed, so the faster wheel receives more power.[^open-principles]

### Why the weaker tyre sets the limit

Equal torque has a catch. Suppose one tyre can take only 100 Nm at its wheel before slipping, while the other could take 600 Nm. In steady, unbraked driving, an ideal open differential can then sustain only about 100 Nm at each wheel: 200 Nm in total. Its freely turning bevel gears cannot hold a large torque difference between the sides, so the grippier tyre's spare capacity goes unused.

These are wheel torques, not engine torque; they already include each tyre's load, the road friction and the tyre radius. A real car adds bearing friction, rotating inertia and electronic intervention, and once a wheel accelerates into wheelspin a simple torque balance no longer applies.

## Mechanical limited slip and brake intervention

The optional **mechanically locking rear differential** adds friction discs that resist the outputs turning relative to the carrier. That lets the two wheels carry different torques, so more drive can go to the better-loaded or grippier tyre when accelerating out of a bend. The wheels can still turn at different speeds in a corner; this is not a solid axle.[^final-drive]

Porsche quotes locking values of **22% under drive and 27% on overrun** for this plate-type unit; drive and overrun load the mechanism differently. The figures describe how strongly it locks, not a fixed share of engine torque sent to one wheel. The torque at each tyre depends on load, grip and the plates' friction behaviour, so the interactive figure models only the open differential rather than inventing a map for this one.[^lock-values]

Standard PSM takes a different route. Its **automatic brake differential (ABD)** brakes a spinning rear wheel, and the extra resistance lets the open differential deliver more torque to the other wheel, though some power becomes heat in the brake. PSM can also cut engine output through its traction control. The [brakes chapter](../13-brakes-and-stability-systems/index.mdx) covers the sensors, hydraulics and wider stability response.[^abd]

Limited slip works inside the transaxle and ABD through the wheel brakes, and a car can have both. Porsche says the mechanical differential reduces how often PSM intervenes in demanding driving. Neither creates grip. Both help the engine's torque use the grip that exists.[^abd]

## Driveshaft joints, plunge and the hub

Each rear driveshaft, or **half-shaft**, runs from a transmission output to a wheel hub. Suspension travel changes its angle and the distance between its joints, and the power unit shifts slightly on its mounts. A rigid shaft fixed at both ends would put unwanted loads on the transmission and wheel bearings.

**Constant-velocity (CV) joints** carry rotation through an angle without the speed fluctuation of a single ordinary universal joint. The shaft needs two separate freedoms: **articulation** for the changing angle, and sliding, or **plunge**, for the changing length. Balls running in tracks inside the joint carry the torque.[^cv]

<Figure src={illustration3} alt={"Original schematic of a driveshaft between a fixed transmission flange and moving wheel, showing joint articulation, axial plunge and the separation between the rotating hub and stationary wheel carrier."}>

Articulation and plunge between a fixed transmission flange and a wheel that moves with the suspension. The hub rotates; the wheel carrier around it does not. In this original schematic, the dashed position is illustrative, not a real joint angle or travel limit.

</Figure>

Folded bellows boots keep the joint grease in and dirt and water out. Because they flex constantly, their condition matters as much as whether the shaft looks straight. Porsche's parts drawing lists the shaft assembly, joint, boots, clips, grease and wheel hub separately. The inner joint bolts to a transmission drive flange; at the outer end, splines engage the hub and a retaining nut secures it. A seal around the drive flange keeps gearbox oil in, separately from the joint grease. The adjoining rear-carrier drawing adds a bearing-retaining cover and a wheel-speed sensor, which measures rotation but carries no drive.[^pet-shafts]

<Figure src={illustration4} alt={"Porsche's exploded parts illustration of a rear driveshaft, splined wheel hub, joint, flexible boots and retaining hardware."}>

Rear driveshaft and hub, PET plate 501-005. The parts list separates manual from PDK, and Boxster from S, applications. Factory illustration: Porsche AG. [Source, PDF p. 187](https://www.porsche-preparation.com/downloaded/pet-porsche/boxster-2009-2012-type-987.pdf#page=187).

</Figure>

The hub turns with the wheel and brake disc on a bearing in the stationary wheel carrier. The bearing carries road loads; the splined shaft delivers torque. A noisy bearing and a worn CV joint are therefore different faults, even if both just sound like a noise from a rear corner. Suspension links locate the carrier, as the [suspension chapter](../11-suspension-and-wheel-geometry/index.mdx) explains.

## Engine and gearbox mounts

The bolted-together engine and gearbox form one power unit. A forward engine support and others at the transmission end carry its weight and resist its tendency to rock under torque. Porsche's catalogue lists the engine bracket and mounting console separately from the transmission supports, which have gearbox-specific listings. At the front support, two stops flank the central mounting, with a separate vibration damper beside it. None of these parts carries drive.[^mounts]

A mount must be soft enough to keep vibration out of the body, yet firm enough to restrain the power unit when you accelerate, change gear or lift off. Whenever torque changes, the casing reacts in the opposite direction to the rotating drive. The mounts flex slightly, pass that reaction into the structure, and their damping helps stop the unit rocking on afterwards.

<Figure src={illustration5} alt={"Porsche's engine-support illustration showing the mounting console, central compliant support, bracket and stops as separate parts."}>

Engine bracket, flexible central support and stops, PET plate 109-000. Factory illustration: Porsche AG. [Source, PDF p. 96](https://www.porsche-preparation.com/downloaded/pet-porsche/boxster-2009-2012-type-987.pdf#page=96).

</Figure>

Firmer mounts can sharpen the response to torque changes but let more vibration into the cabin. Too much movement can spoil refinement and place greater demands on the connected hoses, exhaust joints and driveshaft joints. That is the compromise; it is not advice on aftermarket mounts. The period documents examined show no electronically controlled dynamic mounts or Porsche Torque Vectoring for the 2.9, so those later names do not describe its mounts or ABD.

## Options on the 2010 2.9

The January 2010 UK catalogue lists the mechanical differential as **option 220**, requiring **18- or 19-inch wheels**, so the 2.9's standard 17-inch wheels do not qualify. Larger wheels do not prove the differential was ordered; check the build record as described in the [specification and options chapter](../03-the-2010-boxster-2-9-specification-and-options/index.mdx).[^option-2010]

## Changes in other 2.9 model years

The launch catalogue offered option 220 from **May 2009 at the earliest**, with manual transmission and 18/19-inch wheels.[^option-launch] The January 2010 catalogue keeps the wheel condition but drops the manual-only note. That change of wording does not establish a new eligibility date. The [annual matrix](../19-reference-atlas-and-index/index.mdx#annual-equipment-matrix) records the uncertainty alongside later service-parts evidence.[^pet-shafts]

## The S, Spyder and Black Edition

The **Boxster S** has the same layout and, despite its higher torque, the mechanical differential is still an option in the January 2010 UK table. The parts catalogue lists Boxster and S driveshafts separately.[^option-2010]

The **Spyder** has the locking differential as standard, as part of its sports chassis. On the **Boxster S Black Edition** it remains an extra-cost UK option, even with standard 19-inch Spyder-design wheels.[^derivatives]

## Identification and ageing

Check the differential and driveshaft application against the transaxle actually fitted. Turning a raised wheel by hand proves little, because brake drag and the state of a plate differential both affect the result.

On inspection, look for grease flung from a damaged boot, deteriorating mounts and leaks at the output seals. Note whether a noise follows road speed, cornering load or the change between drive and overrun; a clonk can come from more than one clearance in the drive path. Joint fasteners, hub retention and any work that needs the power unit supported call for the correct workshop information for the parts fitted.
