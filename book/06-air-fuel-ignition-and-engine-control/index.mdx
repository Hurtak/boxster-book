# Air, fuel, ignition and engine control

The 2.9 makes its torque by drawing in air, adding the right amount of petrol and igniting the mixture at the right moment in each cylinder's cycle. These jobs form one controlled process: the accelerator sends a request, sensors report the engine's condition, and the electronics coordinate the throttle, injectors, ignition and inlet-valve control to meet it.

On the 2010 Boxster 2.9, fuel is sprayed into the **inlet ports**, ahead of the inlet valves; the contemporary S injects directly into the cylinders.[^controller] [Engine construction and valve gear](../05-engine-construction-and-valve-gear/index.mdx) explains the mechanical engine and the VarioCam Plus hardware. This chapter is about how the engine's electronics decide what that hardware does.

## Following the air

The air cleaner holds a **cylindrical pleated element** that removes particles before the air reaches the measuring and metering parts. A hot-film mass-air-flow sensor, or **MAF**, then measures the mass of air coming in. Mass is what matters, because the fuel has to match the oxygen available: a given volume of hot, thin air contains less oxygen than the same volume of colder, denser air. Finally, the electronic throttle controls the restriction into the intake system, whose branches feed the two cylinder banks.[^air-cleaner][^maf-principle]

<Figure src={illustration2} alt={"Schematic flow from air filter through MAF and throttle to the resonance intake and the cylinders’ inlet ports."}>

Air passes the filter, MAF, throttle and resonance intake before reaching the inlet ports, where the fuel joins it. The drawing shows the sequence, not the real hose routes.

</Figure>

The intake manifold also exploits the pressure waves set up by the engine's stop-start breathing. Its connected passages and switchable flaps change how the air columns behave as engine speed rises. Rubber sleeves and clamps join the two bank distributors to the centre tube, and two vacuum units, switched by electrically controlled change-over valves, work the flaps. Porsche's 2.9 training material gives the switching points: the distribution-pipe flap opens above 3,800 rpm and the tuning flap above 5,500 rpm, and both are held closed by vacuum at idle. It also describes a redesigned upper resonance chamber that suppresses unwanted resonance noise at high speed. These flaps are separate from the main throttle: the throttle meters the load the driver asks for, while the flaps change how the intake is tuned.[^intake]

## The accelerator asks for torque

The accelerator pedal measures its own movement and sends the signal to the engine controller. The driver's foot supplies an input to a calculation rather than pulling a cable to a fixed throttle angle, and Bosch describes the pedal signal as a request for torque. The controller turns that request into a suitable air charge and coordinates the fuel and spark to burn it.[^pedal]

Inside the electronic throttle body, an electric motor turns a butterfly valve, and a position sensor reports its actual angle so that the controller can compare it with the command. Opening the valve reduces the restriction in the intake. At a given engine speed and operating condition, that generally lets more air into the cylinders and produces more torque. Pedal position, throttle angle and percentage of maximum torque are three different quantities, and the relationship between them also depends on engine speed.[^throttle]

The same arrangement lets other systems influence engine output. Idle control can admit enough air to keep the engine running with no foot on the pedal. A gearbox can coordinate a shift with the engine, and stability control can ask for a change in output. The 2.9's controller specifically supports communication with the optional PDK. [PDK transmission](../09-pdk-transmission/index.mdx) covers the gearbox's side, and [Brakes and stability systems](../13-brakes-and-stability-systems/index.mdx) the driving-dynamics side.[^controller][^throttle]

## Supplying and metering the fuel

The fuel tank sits in the front of the car, well away from the engine. Its electric delivery pump sends filtered, pressure-regulated petrol along the feed line to the engine's fuel distribution pipes. A level sender reports how much fuel is in the tank; it does not measure delivery pressure. Porsche's parts catalogue lists separate pump applications for the 2.9 and the S.[^fuel-supply]

A connected fuel-pipe assembly feeds both banks. It carries six injectors, held by retaining clips and sealed at both their fuel-pipe and inlet-port ends, and each injector meters fuel into its own cylinder's inlet port. The controller sets each dose by how long it holds the injector open, allowing for the operating conditions. *Sequential multipoint injection* means that each cylinder has its own injector, operated in a suitable sequence, rather than one shared carburettor feeding them all.[^port-principle][^later]

For the 2.9, Porsche specifies injectors with eight fine outlet holes, supplied at about **4 bar**. They are mounted in the flange area of the individual intake ports, so the fuel is sprayed upstream of the inlet valve and carried into the cylinder as part of the incoming charge.[^injectors]

Direct injection has a different task. Its injector must deliver fuel straight into a cylinder whose pressure changes throughout the cycle, so the S adds a camshaft-driven high-pressure stage, pressure regulation and suitable injectors. Porsche gives **40–120 bar** for that system, and its spray preparation, piston shape and injection timing are designed together. The high-pressure pump and injector drawings in the ordinary-model brochure are explicitly of the **3.4-litre S**, not the 2.9.[^dfi]

On the 2.9, a **pressure regulator** holds the supply near its intended pressure, while the injector's opening time sets the dose. The two jobs are separate: correctly timed injector pulses can only compensate for a supply error up to a point. The 2.9 has no equivalent of the S's high-pressure stage.[^fuel-supply]

### Tank ventilation and stored vapour

Petrol also evaporates inside the tank. A separate breather line takes the vapour to an **activated-carbon canister**, which stores it. In suitable running conditions, a controlled purge route carries the stored vapour into the intake to be burned, while the air drawn through the canister helps regenerate its capacity. The purge valve lets the engine control manage this extra contribution to the mixture.

This **evaporative-emissions** path carries vapour and air, whereas the supply pipe carries liquid under pressure; neither is the exhaust's secondary-air system. A leak or a stuck purge valve can upset mixture control even when the liquid-fuel feed is intact. The Porsche catalogue lists the canister, breather and purge pipes separately, and emission-market differences need checking on a particular car.[^fuel-vapour]

## Crankcase ventilation and oil separation

Some combustion gas passes the piston rings into the crankcase. This **blow-by** has to leave by a controlled route, so that pressure does not build up around the rotating parts. The ventilation system separates out the oil mist the gas carries, then returns the gas to the intake. Porsche describes a pre-separator and a fine separator in the new engine generation.[^crankcase-vent]

<Figure src={illustration5} alt={"Blow-by enters the crankcase past the piston rings, reaches oil separation, then returns as ventilated gas to the intake. Separated oil returns to the engine’s oil collection; it is not sent through the carbon canister."}>

Blow-by passes the piston rings, goes through oil separation and returns to the intake; the separated oil drains back to the engine, not to the carbon canister. Porsche's factory drawings show separation hardware inside the engine as well as an external oil separator and breather hoses; the single separation block groups them. It does not borrow the earlier 987.1 separator's construction or a diagnostic vacuum limit.

</Figure>

Separating the oil and controlling the pressure are different jobs. Separation removes the suspended oil droplets; a controlled connection manages the crankcase pressure relative to the intake. An unrestricted opening to the intake's depression would not give the same controlled ventilation, which is why the breather hoses and oil separator matter to the intake's airtightness as well as to the oil system. The [oil pumps](../07-lubrication-cooling-and-exhaust/index.mdx#oil-collection-and-pressure-supply) do a separate job: circulating liquid lubricant.

These **crankcase gases** are not the tank vapour described above. Both end up burned via the intake, but they have different origins, separators and control paths.

## Ignition and knock control

Each cylinder has its own ignition coil, sitting directly on its spark plug. While current flows through the coil's primary winding, it stores energy in a magnetic field; switching the current off produces the high voltage that strikes a spark across the plug gap. The spark only starts combustion, and the burning mixture takes a finite time to release its energy, so spark timing must suit engine speed and load rather than coming at the same crank position every time.[^coil-principle]

Porsche's new coils contain their own electronic output stages and diagnostic functions, and the controller triggers each of the six individually. There is no rotating distributor and no set of long high-voltage leads from a central coil. The training document also describes surface-gap plugs with four ground electrodes arranged around the insulator. That describes how the plug is built, not four separately timed sparks.[^ignition][^plug]

Knock is abnormal combustion in which part of the unburned charge ignites spontaneously, causing sharp pressure disturbances. A knock sensor picks up the characteristic vibration this sends through the engine structure. In effect it listens to the engine, unlike the oxygen sensors, which judge the mixture in the exhaust.[^knock-principle]

The 2.9 has one knock sensor on each side of the crankcase. Porsche describes cylinder-selective control: the controller adjusts the timing of a knocking cylinder until the knock stops. If a sensor fails, protective retard under load can reduce power and increase consumption. Knock control manages combustion within the engine's calibrated operating range.[^ignition]

## What the controller needs to know

Crankshaft position gives engine speed and shows where each event belongs in the cycle. Camshaft position relates the inlet-valve system to that reference. Air measurement and temperature describe the incoming charge, and temperature also tells a cold engine from one ready for its normal operating strategies. Throttle feedback confirms whether the actuator actually reached its intended position.[^inputs]

The oxygen sensors report the result of mixture preparation after combustion, and the knock sensors help find the timing limit. Each signal answers a different question: a plausible pedal signal says nothing by itself about fuel pressure, a working spark or an airtight intake. Coordinating them is the controller's main job, and disagreements between them help diagnosis.

## Mixture feedback: checking the result

The 2.9 has **wideband oxygen sensors before the first catalyst brick** and **switching sensors between the two bricks in each bank's converter assembly**. The upstream sensors serve mixture control; the downstream ones help monitor the exhaust treatment. Because each bank has its own exhaust path, the control can respond to differences between the banks.[^controller][^exhaust-sensors]

Lambda, written **λ**, compares the actual air–fuel ratio with the chemically balanced ratio for the fuel. At λ = 1 the air and fuel are in their stoichiometric proportions. Above 1 the mixture is lean, with air to spare; below 1 it is rich. A wideband sensor can measure how far the mixture is from that point on either side, rather than only signalling the switch from rich to lean.[^lambda]

During suitable warm, steady running, feedback compares the measured mixture with its target and trims the fuelling. Suppose the meter reports enough air for a certain quantity of fuel, but a leak downstream admits another ten per cent. The fuel calculation is now too small; the oxygen sensors' response can lead the controller to add fuel. The correction compensates for the error, but the leak remains.[^lambda]

<Diagram id="engine-mixture-feedback" poster={illustration1} alt={"Ten per cent additional unmetered air initially gives lambda 1.100 in this illustrative model. Successive fuel corrections approach lambda one."} />

*An illustrative model, not measured Porsche behaviour. Everything except the leak and the correction is held steady.*

A real engine is rarely that steady: it is starting, accelerating, changing temperature or entering overrun, and its target and available feedback depend on that state. A fuel correction therefore shows that the control has responded, not which part has failed. An air leak, a fuel-delivery fault or misleading sensor information can all disturb the same relationship.

## Cold running and emissions

Cold running changes how the fuel is prepared and how well the exhaust treatment works. Oxygen sensors need suitable operating conditions, and a catalyst must get hot enough to convert pollutants effectively. Sensor heating and warm-up strategies are therefore as much a part of engine management as steady-running mixture control.[^lambda-heating]

One difference matters on this car: the **2.9 retains secondary-air injection**, whereas the contemporary 3.4 DFI engine manages its warm-up emissions without it. Secondary air adds oxygen on the exhaust side during the relevant warm-up phase, helping to burn off unburned components and heat the exhaust treatment. An electric pump sends it through a valve and separate connection lines to each bank's exhaust passages, an air path quite separate from the charge entering through the inlet valves. Porsche's training material confirms Euro 5 compliance for the European version.[^secondary]

The S can use start and warm-up strategies specific to direct injection, including multiple injection and altered injection timing. These do not apply to the 2.9. The catalysts and exhaust layout are covered in [Lubrication, cooling and exhaust](../07-lubrication-cooling-and-exhaust/index.mdx).[^warmup-dfi]

## Options on the 2010 2.9

**Sport Chrono** changes how the engine responds. In Sport mode, a given part-way pedal movement asks for more output, so the engine feels more immediate. Porsche also describes a harder rev-limiter characteristic. Its published comparison curves meet at maximum requested torque, however: the option does not give the 188 kW / 255 PS engine a higher peak-power rating.[^chrono]

This port-injected engine and its ME 7.8.2 controller belong to the 2009 introduction. ## The S, Spyder and Black Edition

All three use 3.4-litre direct injection, with the high-pressure fuel stage described above. Their hardware and calibration differ from the 2.9's port system; [engine construction](../05-engine-construction-and-valve-gear/index.mdx#the-s-spyder-black-edition-and-cayman) compares their outputs and internal dimensions.[^variants]

## Identification and ageing

Useful observations tie a symptom to the conditions in which it appears: cold or warm, idling or under load, one bank or both, intermittent or repeatable. Intake joints, electrical connections, coils, plugs and fuel delivery all take part in the same process. Stored fault information and measured values help tell a failed actuator from the controller compensating for a problem elsewhere.
