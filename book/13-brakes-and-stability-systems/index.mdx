# Brakes and stability systems

Braking turns motion into heat. Press the pedal of a 2010 Boxster 2.9 and fluid pressure rises, pads clamp the discs, and the tyres' grip on the road slows the car. Alongside that physical chain, electronic systems watch the wheels and the car's movement, and can adjust individual brakes when needed.

## The brakes on the 2010 Boxster 2.9

The reference car has hydraulic, power-assisted brakes with cast-iron discs at all four wheels. The discs are internally ventilated and cross-drilled, and each is gripped by a black, fixed aluminium monobloc caliper with four pistons. A stationary cover plate sits behind each disc, separate hardware retains the pads, and electrical leads serve their wear indicators. The wheel brakes are the same with the six-speed manual or optional PDK.[^hardware]

| Component | 2010 Boxster 2.9 |
| --- | --- |
| Front discs | 318 mm diameter × 28 mm nominal thickness |
| Rear discs | 299 mm diameter × 20 mm nominal thickness |
| Calipers | Four-piston aluminium monobloc fixed calipers, front and rear |
| Original caliper finish | Black anodised |
| Stability control | Porsche Stability Management, or PSM, fitted as standard |
| Parking brake | A hand lever operating the rear-wheel parking brakes through cables |

These are new-disc dimensions, not wear limits. A worn disc must be checked against the specification for that particular part.[^hardware]

The master cylinder and fluid reservoir are at the front. Rigid pipes and flexible hoses carry fluid to the wheels; the hoses let the wheels steer and move with the suspension without a rigid pipe bending repeatedly.

## How a stop happens

<Figure src={illustration3} alt={"The braking chain: foot force is assisted and converted to hydraulic pressure; calipers clamp the rotating discs, and road force on the tyres slows the car."}>

Each stage changes the form of the effort: the booster adds to the driver's foot, the master cylinder turns force into pressure, the calipers turn it back into clamping force, and the road finally does the slowing.

</Figure>

### From your foot to hydraulic pressure

The pedal is a lever, and a brake booster adds to the driver's effort. The master cylinder turns the combined force into pressure in the brake fluid. The brake lines carry that pressure to the caliper pistons, whose area turns it back into force on the pads. A small movement of fluid produces a large clamping force.

The Boxster has two hydraulic circuits, split between the front and rear axles. If one fails the other still works, though braking is seriously reduced. The driver's connection to the brakes stays hydraulic and mechanical; the electronics add control of pressure within that system.[^handbook]

Passing on pressure this way depends on the correct fluid in a sound, properly bled circuit. Liquid barely compresses; air or vapour does, and uses up pedal travel before the pads clamp as hard as expected.

### Where brake assistance comes from

The booster's assistance comes from a mechanical **vacuum pump** on the rear engine, driven by the exhaust camshaft on bank 4–6. It evacuates the booster supply and serves other vacuum-operated functions, and a long line carries the low pressure forward to the booster at the master cylinder. The pump is separate from the accessory belt and from the hydraulic ABS/PSM pump.[^vacuum-source]

<Figure src={illustration4} alt={"The exhaust camshaft drives a rear vacuum pump; a supply line and non-return valve serve the front booster. Pedal operation admits atmospheric pressure to one booster chamber so a pressure difference assists the pushrod into the hydraulic master cylinder."}>

Vacuum is made at the back of the car and used at the front. Pressing the pedal admits outside air to one booster chamber, and the pressure difference provides the assisting push.

</Figure>

Inside a vacuum booster of this general type, a diaphragm separates two chambers; the pedal's valves let atmospheric pressure act on one side while the other stays evacuated. A non-return valve holds the vacuum when the source pressure changes. The booster reduces leg effort, but the driver's foot still commands the braking.[^booster-principle]

The vacuum line carries air, not fluid, so losing vacuum makes the pedal much harder without being a hydraulic failure. After the engine stops, only a limited reserve of assistance remains. [The options section](#options-on-the-2010-2.9) separates this ordinary booster from the additional hydraulic brake-assist function.

### What the four-piston caliper does

A fixed caliper straddles the disc without moving, with pistons on both sides. As pressure rises, they push the two pads against opposite faces of the spinning disc, and the caliper body carries the reaction forces.

Disc (1), cover (3), caliper (5), pads (6) and wear lead (7) are separate items. The plate is shared with other brake versions: the external connecting tube (10) belongs to PCCB, not the 2.9's standard iron-disc brakes. Factory drawing: Porsche AG, [PET 602-000, PDF p. 200](https://www.porsche-preparation.com/downloaded/pet-porsche/boxster-2009-2012-type-987.pdf#page=200).

</Figure>

*Monobloc* means the body is made as one main piece. Its stiffness limits flexing that would absorb fluid movement and soften the link between pressure and clamping force. The piston count is only part of the story: piston area, pads, disc geometry, cooling and tyres matter too.[^calipers]

The pads carry the friction material. Its formulation decides how readily the brakes bite, how friction changes with temperature, and how much noise, dust and wear they produce.[^friction] As the pads wear, the pistons rest farther out to keep a small working clearance, so the reservoir level can fall slightly in normal use. The wear-indicator leads are electrical wires, separate from the hydraulic hoses and from the wheel-speed sensors used by ABS and PSM.[^handbook]

### From clamping force to heat

Friction between pad and disc creates a torque against the wheel's rotation. The road then pushes rearwards on the tyres, slowing the car, as the tyres push forwards on the road. The disc absorbs much of the heat and passes it to the air and neighbouring parts.

That energy rises steeply with speed. A round-figure 1,400 kg car carries about **540 kilojoules** of straight-line kinetic energy at 100 km/h, and about **2,160 kilojoules** at 200 km/h: four times as much. These are illustrative sums using one-half mass times speed squared, not Boxster stopping tests, and they leave out rotating parts, drag and other losses.

<Diagram id="braking-energy" poster={illustration2} alt={"For an illustrative 1,400 kg car, kinetic energy rises from about 540 kJ at 100 km/h to about 2,160 kJ at 200 km/h. Doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy."} />

A harder stop releases that energy faster, and repeated stops add heat before the last has escaped, so a brake needs thermal capacity as well as torque.

The ventilated discs have an air gap between their friction faces, crossed by internal passages, and rotation moves cooling air through them. Cross-drilling is a different feature: holes through the friction surfaces. The car also directs cooling air towards its brakes.[^brochure]

### Why the front and rear brakes differ

Braking transfers load onto the front axle, so the front tyres can take more of the braking force while the rears are lightly loaded. The larger front discs suit that balance: the farther out the pads act, the more leverage they have on the wheel. Mass and construction also govern how a disc absorbs and sheds heat, so diameter alone is an incomplete description.

The tyre's grip sets the final limit: near lock-up, extra hydraulic pressure no longer turns into proportionally more useful road force. That is where the electronics come in.

## ABS and PSM

ABS and PSM share hardware but do different jobs. ABS manages wheel slip under braking. PSM considers the whole car's movement and can brake individual wheels to influence its direction.

### ABS keeps the wheels turning

Each wheel has a speed sensor. When a wheel starts slowing towards lock-up, valves in the hydraulic unit hold or reduce its pressure, then let it rise again as the wheel recovers. The cycle repeats as conditions change.[^abs]

A rolling tyre can still generate steering force, so ABS helps preserve directional control in a heavy stop, within the grip the tyres and road can offer. Its operation shows as a rapid pulsing in the pedal and audible hydraulic activity, which is normal. Porsche's handbook tells the driver to keep firm, continuous pressure on the pedal in an emergency stop rather than pump it.[^handbook]

### PSM watches the car's direction

Porsche Stability Management compares the course the driver asks for, read from the steering, with the one the car is taking. Wheel speeds, sideways acceleration and the car's rate of rotation about its vertical axis, called *yaw*, show what it is actually doing.

Braking one wheel creates a turning effect on the car. If the car departs from the intended path, PSM can use that effect, together with engine-torque control, to help stabilise it. Electronic stability systems share this principle.[^stability]

If the rear starts to rotate too far on the way into a bend, PSM can brake a suitable wheel to oppose it, and the driver may notice a flashing light, hydraulic noise or reduced engine response. Which wheel it brakes depends on the situation; there is no fixed choice for every slide.

PSM also provides **anti-slip regulation (ASR)** to limit driven-wheel spin, and **automatic brake differential (ABD)** to brake a spinning driven wheel. The [drivetrain chapter](../10-differential-driveshafts-and-mounts/index.mdx#mechanical-limited-slip-and-brake-intervention) explains ABD's torque-transfer effect and the separate mechanical locking differential.[^brochure]

### Preparing for an urgent stop

The facelift added two assistance functions. **Prefilling**, also called precharging, reacts to a very rapid release of the accelerator, which suggests braking may follow. It brings the pads into light contact with the discs, taking up the clearance in advance.

**Brake assist** recognises an emergency stop from the speed and force of the pedal application. The PSM hydraulic unit then adds pressure for maximum deceleration, on top of the ordinary vacuum booster. Prefilling is about readiness; brake assist is about pressure during the stop. Both act through the hydraulic system, subject to the PSM and Sport-mode conditions below.[^assistance]

Hill-start assistance is a separate function. The **Drive-Off Assistant**, provided with manual and PDK gearboxes, briefly holds brake pressure as the driver moves off on a slope. The hold lasts about two seconds at most and releases as the car moves away. The handbook requires a stationary car, engine running, sufficient brake pressure, a gradient of around 5% or more and a gear selected for going up the slope; a manual car also needs the clutch pedal pressed.[^hill]

### The parking brake has its own job

The hand lever pulls separate left and right cables to the rear wheels. There, expanding mechanisms press dedicated shoes against drum surfaces inside the rear discs. The shoes, springs and adjuster are separate from the hydraulic caliper and its pads, so the car stays held without hydraulic pressure.[^parking]

The handbrake, the [PDK parking lock](../09-pdk-transmission/index.mdx#the-selector-and-steering-wheel-controls) and the brief hill hold are three separate ways of stopping the car rolling, each with its own mechanism and conditions.

## Options on the 2010 2.9

ABS and PSM are standard. The relevant option is Sport Chrono, code **639**, or **640** in Plus form with PCM.[^options]

For the brakes, it changes the control strategy, not the iron-disc dimensions. Sport mode raises the PSM intervention threshold, allowing more movement before PSM steps in. [Chapter 18](../18-occupant-protection-and-system-integration/index.mdx#options-on-the-2010-2.9) connects the equipment-dependent mode changes.[^options]

With **PSM OFF**, ABS and ABD remain available. Stability intervention returns during braking when either front wheel enters ABS regulation, or both front wheels if Sport mode is selected. The handbook says the additional braking functions are deactivated with PSM off, with emergency reactivation tied to PSM intervention. The technical introduction adds that selecting SPORT with Sport Chrono Package Plus deactivates the additional hydraulic brake assist. The ordinary vacuum booster keeps assisting the pedal throughout.[^modes]

**Porsche Ceramic Composite Brake (PCCB)** was an S option, not available on the ordinary 2.9 in the UK catalogue. Its hardware is compared below.[^options]

## Changes in other 2.9 model years

The 2009 facelift enlarged the ordinary model's front discs from **298 to 318 mm** and introduced the PSM prefill and brake-assist functions, both before the reference car.[^introduction] The later parts catalogue lists a new hydraulic unit from model year 2010 and a new control-system sensor from model year 2011, but those splits say nothing about a change in braking behaviour. Later US confirmation of the diameters and the unresolved UK running details are in the [annual matrix](../19-reference-atlas-and-index/index.mdx#annual-equipment-matrix).[^later]

The S, Spyder and Black Edition

</Heading>

The more powerful 987.2 models share the 2.9's general braking architecture. Comparing their standard iron brakes shows one dimensional difference.

| Model | Front disc | Rear disc | Caliper colour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Boxster 2.9 | 318 × 28 mm | 299 × 20 mm | Black |
| Boxster S | 318 × 28 mm | 299 × 24 mm | Red |
| Boxster Spyder | 318 × 28 mm | 299 × 24 mm | Red |
| Boxster S Black Edition | 318 × 28 mm | 299 × 24 mm | Red |

That difference is at the **rear**, where the S disc is 4 mm thicker; the fronts match. Extra thickness can add thermal capacity, but matching dimensions do not prove that calipers, pads or assemblies interchange. The later parts catalogue lists common standard front-disc numbers but separate base and S front-caliper numbers. Porsche's technical introduction gives the Cayman 2.9 and Cayman S the same dimensions as their Boxster equivalents, another shared application of the design within the 987 family.[^hardware]

The Spyder's dimensions come from its German price list. Its brakes also have a lighter car to stop, and braking depends on mass as well as engine output. The UK Black Edition price list independently gives the S disc dimensions, and its brochure confirms red four-piston monobloc calipers. Neither proves that every part interchanges.[^special]

### What PCCB changes

The S could be ordered with ceramic-composite discs and yellow calipers, and PCCB is also an option in the Spyder and UK Black Edition catalogues. The ordinary UK catalogue describes 350 mm discs at both axles, six-piston front calipers and four-piston rear calipers: a complete system of matching discs, calipers and pads.[^pccb]

The lighter discs reduce rotating mass and [unsprung mass](../11-suspension-and-wheel-geometry/index.mdx#compliance-and-unsprung-mass). The ceramic-composite material also behaves differently with heat and wear.

Identification and ageing

</Heading>

### Which brakes are fitted

Caliper colour is only a first clue: a previous owner may have repainted the calipers, fitted replacement discs or changed the whole system. Disc part numbers and the correct dimensional specification are stronger evidence. The wear lip at a disc's outer edge makes an improvised measurement a poor substitute for a proper inspection.

<Heading level={3} id="ageing-and-wear">

Ageing and wear

</Heading>

Pads and discs wear, fluid absorbs moisture and exposed metal corrodes, so a car that covers few miles can still need brake work.

Light surface rust after rain or washing is different from established corrosion, heavy pitting or an uneven working surface. Porsche's handbook notes that cast-iron discs can corrode during extended parking. Check both friction faces; the wheel hides much of the inner one.[^handbook]

Heat causes two distinct problems. **Friction fade** is a loss of useful friction when the pad/disc interface gets too hot.[^friction] **Fluid boiling** puts compressible vapour into a system that relies on liquid. Each can feel different at the pedal.[^fluid]

Brake fluid is hygroscopic: it absorbs moisture over time, which lowers its boiling point. Fluid age therefore matters even when the pads are thick. Follow the maintenance requirements for the car and its use for fluid specification and replacement timing.[^fluid]

Seals and flexible hoses age too. A leak, restricted movement or a piston that fails to release can affect pressure or leave a pad dragging, so compare the wear on both pads and both faces.

The parking brake has its own shoes, cables and adjustment, so fresh rear pads say little about it. Equally, a wheel-speed signal fault can affect the electronic assistance while the discs and pads look sound. Mechanical hardware and electronic control need to be checked together.
