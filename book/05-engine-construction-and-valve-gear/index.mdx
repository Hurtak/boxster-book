# Engine construction and valve gear

The 2010 European Boxster 2.9 has a water-cooled, naturally aspirated flat-six mounted behind the seats and ahead of the rear axle. Its cylinders lie flat, across the car, rather than standing upright. Six pistons turn a single crankshaft, and four camshafts work the valves that let the fresh charge in and the exhaust out.

The engine belongs to the new generation introduced with the 2009 facelift. It is **port-injected**, whereas the contemporary Boxster S uses direct injection. It also does without the earlier engine's intermediate shaft. The two facts are independent: an engine can have the new timing drive without having direct injection.[^engine-id][^specification]

### What “flat-six” describes

The crankshaft runs down the middle of the engine, with three cylinders on each side and a cylinder head on the outside of each bank. Opposite cylinders are slightly staggered along the crankshaft, because their connecting rods work on different crank throws. Each piston moves out towards its own head and back towards the crankshaft.

Opposing the cylinders helps balance the piston forces and lets the engine sit low in the car. Combustion still delivers torque in pulses, so the engine mounts isolate the resulting vibration from the body.[^flat]

<Figure src={illustration4} alt={"A plan of the 2010 2.9 shows three cylinders on either side of the crankshaft, connecting rods between pistons and crank throws, and two camshafts in each outer cylinder head."}>

The 2.9 seen from above, with its parts spread apart for clarity: three cylinders either side of the crankshaft, a connecting rod from each piston to its crank throw, and two camshafts in each outer cylinder head.

</Figure>

The real engine is much harder to read. Porsche's parts drawings show two crankcase halves, with separate head gaskets and head covers, around this mechanism. Once the intake, wiring, coolant passages and accessories are in place, most of it is hidden. A photograph from above shows the breathing equipment rather than the pistons that give the engine its name.

## Four strokes turn fuel into rotation

Follow one cylinder. On the **intake** stroke the piston moves away from the head, drawing in a fresh air–fuel charge. On the **compression** stroke it returns with the valves closed, and ignition starts combustion near the end. On the **power** stroke the expanding gas drives the piston away again, and on the **exhaust** stroke it returns while the open exhaust valves let the spent gas out.[^cycle]

The connecting rod joins the piston pin to an offset crank pin. As the crank turns, the rod's angle changes, converting back-and-forth movement into rotation, much as a cyclist's legs turn the pedals. The other cylinders work at different points in their cycles, so the engine delivers a succession of power strokes.

<Diagram id="engine-four-stroke" poster={illustration1} alt={"Intake, compression, power and exhaust occupy two crankshaft revolutions. The crank turns 720 degrees while each camshaft turns 360 degrees; the illustrated valve events are simplified to the stroke boundaries."} />

A complete cycle takes **two crankshaft revolutions**, or 720°. The piston reaches the head end twice, between compression and power and between exhaust and intake, so its position alone cannot identify the stroke. The valves' state and the engine's place in the two-turn cycle matter too.

*Top dead centre* and *bottom dead centre*, the two ends of piston travel, still apply to this horizontal engine: “top” means the head end, not the highest point above the road. The interactive figure turns one cylinder upright to make the movement easier to follow.

Real valves do not snap open at the stroke boundaries. Opening takes time and moving gas has momentum, so the valve events stretch across the simple divisions of the cycle. VarioCam Plus adjusts part of that breathing process.

## Bore, stroke, torque and power

**Bore** is the cylinder's internal diameter; **stroke** is the distance the piston travels between its end positions. The 2.9's 89 mm bore is larger than its 77.5 mm stroke. One cylinder's circular area multiplied by the stroke gives its swept volume, and six of those make the engine's displacement.[^dimensions]

The **11.5:1 compression ratio** compares the largest volume enclosed in the cylinder with the smallest. Torque is turning effect; power is the rate at which work is done. Because power depends on speed as well as torque, the engine can produce more power at higher revs even after its torque has started to fall. Maximum torque is available on a plateau from 4,400 to 6,000 rpm, and [gearing](../08-manual-transmission-and-clutch/index.mdx#ratios-exchange-speed-for-torque) decides the resulting drive at the tyres.[^compression]

**6 × π/4 × 89² × 77.5 ÷ 1,000 ≈ 2,893 cm³.** This is the swept volume only; it leaves out the chamber space that remains with the piston at the head end of its travel. The compression ratio is a ratio of volumes, whereas a compression test measures pressure under its own test conditions.[^dimensions]

**Power in kW = torque in Nm × rpm ÷ approximately 9,550.** At 290 Nm and 6,000 rpm that gives about 182 kW. The separate 188 kW peak at 6,400 rpm implies about 281 Nm at that speed. These are calculations from catalogue figures, not new dynamometer measurements.

## Crankcase, crankshaft, rods and pistons

The 2.9's crankcase is made of aluminium-silicon alloy and split vertically into two halves. Porsche calls the alloy **Alusil**. Its cylinder surfaces are prepared to expose silicon particles, and the pistons and rings run directly on them; there are no conventional cast-in iron liners.[^crankcase]

The crankcase locates the cylinders, carries the crankshaft bearings and contains passages for oil and coolant. It must keep pistons and bearings accurately aligned despite changing temperatures and combustion loads.

The crankshaft is forged and runs in eight main-bearing positions. The connecting rods are forged too. Their big ends are fracture-split, which leaves each rod and its cap with matching surfaces, and each big end carries replaceable bearing shells. The pistons are forged light alloy, each with a pack of three rings and a pin held by securing rings. Porsche distinguishes the conventional crown of the port-injected 2.9 from the crown shape that the S engine needs for direct injection.[^rotating]

The piston crown faces the combustion chamber. The rings seal in the gas pressure and control the oil on the cylinder wall, and the piston pin lets the rod pivot as the crank turns. Two different kinds of contact are at work. The big-end and main bearings carry rotating parts on lubricated bearing surfaces, while the piston and rings slide against the bore.

The engine also needs lubrication and temperature control, and the valve gear and timing drive use oil as a working fluid. [Lubrication, cooling and exhaust](../07-lubrication-cooling-and-exhaust/index.mdx) covers these supporting systems.

## Cylinder heads and valves

Each cylinder has two inlet valves and two exhaust valves, **24 valves in total**. Each head carries an inlet camshaft and an exhaust camshaft, making four camshafts in all. The cam lobes press on hydraulic bucket tappets, which push the valves open; springs close the valves again as the lobes turn away. Hydraulic valve-clearance compensation takes up the working clearance in the valve train.[^heads]

The cylinder head forms the roof of each combustion chamber and carries the inlet and exhaust passages, the valve guides and the seats. Each valve must open to let gas flow, then seal against its seat so that compression and combustion pressure act on the piston.

Porsche's new head design builds the tappet guides into the head itself, in place of the earlier separate guide housing. A head gasket seals each head to the crankcase, and a separate cover with a moulded seal closes the camshaft side. “Four valves per cylinder” is only part of the story: the shape of the passages, the valve sizes, the cam motion and the combustion chamber all affect how well a cylinder fills and empties.[^head-construction]

The 2.9's port injectors sit at the flange area of the individual intake ports, upstream of the inlet valves. [Fuel supply and metering](../06-air-fuel-ignition-and-engine-control/index.mdx#supplying-and-metering-the-fuel) explains what they do there.[^port]

## Chains drive the camshafts

The camshafts turn at **half crankshaft speed**, giving one set of valve events for every two-turn cycle. Two cam-drive chains carry the drive from the crankshaft, one for each bank. Each inlet camshaft has an adjuster unit at its drive wheel; each exhaust camshaft has a fixed sprocket. Guide and tensioning rails control the chains' path, and two hydraulic tensioners, fed from the oil circuit, keep them at the right tension.[^chains]

<Figure src={illustration5} alt={"A connection schematic shows the crankshaft driving two chains and four camshafts, with guide rails and oil-fed hydraulic tensioners. There is no intermediate shaft between crankshaft and camshafts."}>

How the crankshaft drives the four camshafts: two chains, steered by guide rails and kept taut by oil-fed hydraulic tensioners. There is no intermediate shaft between crankshaft and camshafts. The separate oil-pump chain is not shown.

</Figure>

The chain links carry the force, the guides fix the route, and the tensioners press on the rails to take up slack. The sprockets set the two-to-one speed ratio. A third, separate chain drives the oil pump, with its own guide and tensioning blade.

The new drive does without the earlier **intermediate shaft** and its bearing arrangement, but its own chains, guides, bearings and seals are still working parts, and they still wear.

## The external accessory drive

The belt visible outside the engine is not a camshaft timing belt; it drives the accessories. This **double-sided six-rib poly-V belt** takes drive from the crankshaft pulley, which incorporates a torsional vibration damper, to the alternator, steering pump, air-conditioning compressor and coolant pump. Ribs on both faces let it reach the surrounding pulleys.[^accessory-drive]

<Figure src={illustration6} alt={"A functional accessory-drive map connects the crankshaft pulley and common belt to four loads: alternator, hydraulic steering pump, A/C compressor and coolant pump. A hydraulic tensioner controls the belt."}>

One belt, kept under load by a hydraulic tensioner, drives the alternator, hydraulic steering pump, A/C compressor and coolant pump. The drawing shows connections, not real pulley positions or wrap angles.

</Figure>

The tensioner keeps the belt loaded and its damping limits flutter. Because the belt drives by grip, its condition and tension affect several systems at once. A turning pulley does not prove the unit behind it is working: the [clutchless A/C compressor](../15-cabin-seating-and-climate/index.mdx#two-heat-circuits-one-stream-of-cabin-air) can turn while delivering no useful cooling.

The **vacuum pump is camshaft-driven**, outside the belt circuit. Its role is explained with [the brake booster](../13-brakes-and-stability-systems/index.mdx#where-brake-assistance-comes-from).[^accessory-drive]

## VarioCam Plus: timing and lift

VarioCam Plus is standard on the 2.9. It makes two separate inlet-side adjustments: **timing**, when the inlet valves open and close relative to the crankshaft, and **lift**, how far they open.[^variocam]

To change the timing, a camshaft phase adjuster turns the inlet camshaft relative to its drive. Porsche describes an electro-hydraulic vane mechanism with a **40° crank-angle adjustment range**. The engine controller compares the requested phase with the measured camshaft position and controls the hydraulic adjustment.[^phase]

Advancing the inlet cam moves its opening and closing earlier relative to the crank, and so relative to piston movement and the exhaust events; retarding moves them later. This does not make the camshaft run continuously faster than its normal half-crankshaft speed. Its phase changes during the adjustment, and then the new relationship holds.

The 2.9's two documented peak inlet lifts are **3.6 mm and 10.0 mm**. Switching lift selects a different cam-controlled motion for the valves. A larger opening can let more air through, while a smaller one suits other operating conditions. How much air actually enters still depends on pressure differences, how long the valve stays open, the port shape and engine speed.[^lift]

<Diagram id="engine-timing-lift" poster={illustration2} alt={"Moving a valve event earlier changes its timing, while switching from 3.6 to 10.0 millimetres changes peak inlet lift. Curves and durations are illustrative, not a measured Porsche valve map."} />

*Timing moves the whole valve event earlier or later; lift changes its height. The curves are illustrative, not a measured Porsche valve map.*

The controller chooses a combination according to operating conditions, including speed, load and temperature. Porsche describes low lift with advanced timing at low and medium loads, and high lift for high torque and output. High lift can also be used, with different timing, while the engine warms up. There is no single rpm at which “VarioCam comes on”.[^variocam]

## Options on the 2010 2.9

VarioCam Plus and the 188 kW engine are standard. The January 2010 UK catalogue offers no alternative bore, stroke, pistons or valve gear for the 2.9. The options around the engine include [Sport Chrono's engine-control functions](../06-air-fuel-ignition-and-engine-control/index.mdx#options-on-the-2010-2.9) and the [sports exhaust](../07-lubrication-cooling-and-exhaust/index.mdx#options-on-the-2010-2.9). ## The S, Spyder, Black Edition and Cayman

The ordinary 987.2 **Boxster S** has 3,436 cm³, from a 97 mm bore and the same 77.5 mm stroke. It adds DFI, has a 12.5:1 compression ratio, and produces 228 kW / 310 PS and 360 Nm. Porsche's introduction calls this Boxster engine **MA121**. Both engines have VarioCam Plus, but the S's high inlet lift is 10.5 mm against the 2.9's 10.0 mm. The parts catalogue also lists separate crankcase, piston, head and camshaft applications for the 2.9 and the S. A replacement part shared elsewhere does not make the complete engines interchangeable.[^s]

The **Spyder** and **Boxster S Black Edition** also have 3.4-litre DFI engines, rated in Europe at 235 kW / 320 PS at 7,200 rpm and 370 Nm at 4,750 rpm. That higher output is specific to those two derivatives. A full comparison of their internal parts and engine-code suffixes has not been established here.[^special]

The Cayman's related 2.9 is designated **MA120C** and rated at **195 kW / 265 PS**, against the Boxster's 188 kW / 255 PS. Match engines and internal parts by their documented application, not by capacity alone.[^engine-id]

## Identification and ageing

The installed type and serial details identify the engine. Porsche designates the Boxster 2.9 **MA120**, distinct from the Cayman's **MA120C**.[^engine-id] The code says nothing about wear. If the engine makes a noise or logs a fault, investigate the chain drive, bearings and oil-fed adjusters against what is actually observed.
