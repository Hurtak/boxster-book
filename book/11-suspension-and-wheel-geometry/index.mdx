# Suspension and wheel geometry

The standard **2010 Boxster 2.9** has independent suspension with coil-over McPherson struts at both ends. Coil springs carry the body, hydraulic dampers control its movement, and aluminium arms and links guide the wheels. Front and rear anti-roll bars connect movement across each axle. Electronically adjustable damping was an option, rather than part of this reference car's standard suspension.[^suspension-layout]

The engineering problem is more demanding than making a car feel firm. A wheel needs freedom to follow a bump, yet its direction and inclination must remain controlled when the tyre also transmits braking, driving and cornering forces. Meanwhile, the occupants should experience much less movement than the wheel. Suspension reconciles those competing demands through geometry, springing, damping and carefully chosen flexibility.

## How a wheel is held

### At the front

The front wheel carrier holds the hub and its bearing. Above it, the strut rises to an upper mounting in the body. Below it, a lateral lower control arm joins the front crossmember through its inboard bush. A separate fore-and-aft guide runs from the arm to a forward body mounting. The steering tie rod supplies another constraint: moving it turns the carrier about its steering axis. Ball joints permit the necessary angular movement while transmitting force.

Porsche describes a **strut suspension with a trailing link and wishbone**. The wishbone is the lower arm; the strut supplies wheel guidance that an upper arm would provide in a double-wishbone layout.[^suspension-front]

The spring surrounds the damper, but the two do different jobs. The complete strut also carries structural loads between the carrier and upper mounting. At the front, a separate thrust bearing beneath the top mount accommodates rotation as the wheel steers. It is distinct from the hub bearing that allows the wheel to spin. A worn mounting or bearing can consequently affect noise isolation or the way steering movement feels.

The front hub has a central retaining bolt and nut, rather than a driveshaft bringing engine torque to it. A wheel-speed sensor attaches to the carrier; its signal serves the [braking and stability systems](../13-brakes-and-stability-systems/index.mdx). The carrier therefore supports both the rotating hub and a stationary sensor.

### At the rear

The rear wheels also use McPherson-type struts, lower arms and fore-and-aft guidance, with a separate link controlling toe. There is no steering rack commanding these wheels. Instead, their direction must remain suitably controlled as travel and tyre loads change. The driveshaft passes into each rear hub while its joints allow the wheel to move relative to the drivetrain.

Porsche explicitly calls this a McPherson rear axle in its technical introduction. Its parts catalogue separately identifies the rear wishbone, guide and track rod. The guide joins the lower arm; the toe-control rod joins a separate point on the carrier. Those names describe different pieces of wheel location, not three alternative names for the whole suspension. *Toe-angle control* in a specification sheet should likewise not be read as powered rear-wheel steering.[^suspension-rear]

The rear mounting structure has side pieces, a crossmember and bracing. Separate eccentric bolts at the lower-arm and toe-link mountings provide offset pivot locations for setting geometry. They are mechanical mounting features; there is no actuator steering the rear wheels.

The [hub bearing](../10-differential-driveshafts-and-mounts/index.mdx#driveshaft-joints-plunge-and-the-hub) permits wheel rotation; suspension ball joints instead let the guiding links change angle. Both contribute to secure wheel location.

## Springs support; dampers settle

On the standard 2010 2.9, a coil spring remains compressed at rest and provides the main support for the body load at that corner, acting through the suspension geometry. Added luggage or a passenger changes that balance. During a bump the wheel moves upwards relative to the body and the spring compresses farther, storing energy. As it expands again, it returns energy to the moving suspension.

**Spring rate** describes how much extra force is required for an extra amount of compression. It is different from spring free length, and neither measurement alone defines the car's ride height. Installed geometry, spring seats, vehicle load and the rest of the spring specification also matter. The effective rate at the tyre is influenced by the relationship between wheel movement and spring movement.

Without sufficient damping, stored spring energy would keep exchanging with the motion of the body and wheel, producing repeated oscillations. The hydraulic damper resists relative movement by forcing oil through controlled passages and valves, converting mechanical energy into heat. **Compression**, often called bump, shortens the unit; **rebound** extends it. The resistance can differ between the two directions.[^suspension-damping]

Damper force depends on movement, particularly piston velocity. That is the speed at which the damper changes length, not the car's road speed. A slow-moving car meeting a sharp edge can produce rapid damper movement. A fast car crossing a gentle undulation may produce a much slower stroke. “High-speed damping” is therefore an easily misunderstood phrase.

Porsche describes the ordinary spring struts as coil springs combined with double-tube gas-filled dampers. The gas charge helps the hydraulic unit operate consistently; the metal coil spring remains the principal element supporting the car. The parts drawings distinguish its seats and isolators from the protective bellows and compressible auxiliary stop around the damper rod. The bellows protects the rod; the auxiliary stop helps manage loading near the end of compression travel.[^suspension-struts]

<Diagram id="suspension-spring-damper" poster={illustration1} alt={"Two springs with equal compression carry equal additional spring force. A stationary ideal damper produces no force; a moving damper resists its motion. Position and velocity can therefore change independently."} />

In the interactive example, hold the spring compressed and set velocity to zero. Spring force remains, while the ideal damper force disappears. Then reverse the velocity without changing compression. The damper's resisting direction reverses. This simplified experiment separates two causes that occur together on a moving car; its displayed rates are teaching values, not Porsche measurements.

## What the anti-roll bars add

Each anti-roll bar is a torsion spring connecting the left and right suspension through links. Each drop link joins a bar arm to a strut; separate bushes and brackets support the bar on the axle structure and allow the central section to rotate. When both wheels move together by the same amount in an ideal symmetric arrangement, the bar largely rotates without additional twist. The individual road springs still resist that movement.

When one wheel rises relative to the other, the bar's arms move differently and twist its central section. The resulting resistance opposes that difference. In a bend, where the outside suspension tends to compress and the inside tends to extend, this contributes to roll stiffness. On a one-wheel bump, it also couples some of the disturbance to the other side.

Increasing roll stiffness is consequently a compromise, not a free increase in grip. How it is distributed between front and rear affects how cornering load transfer is shared between the axles. The tyres, springs, geometry and road surface all participate in the result. A visibly flatter body alone does not prove greater available cornering grip.

## Compliance and unsprung mass

Rubber bushes allow controlled flexibility at selected connections and reduce vibration reaching the body. Their stiffness helps determine how much the connection moves under load.

Under braking or cornering load, small elastic movements can change wheel position and toe. This is **compliance**, distinct from the geometry change caused by deliberate suspension travel. Splitting, separation or excessive movement can let the tyre steer the wheel more than intended. A car may then feel unsettled even when a stationary alignment reading appears plausible.

The body, passengers and powertrain are mainly sprung mass. Wheels, tyres, hubs, brake assemblies and wheel carriers contribute to **unsprung mass**, moving more directly with the road. Portions of the arms and driveshafts contribute too; the boundary is not simply every part below the spring. Less mass following a sharp road disturbance can make the wheel's movement easier to control, but the outcome still depends on tyres and damping.

Porsche uses aluminium extensively in the moving suspension structure. This serves the effort to keep mass down without making the wheel-guiding structure arbitrarily flexible. A larger-diameter wheel is not automatically lighter, and a claimed reduction should be based on the complete fitted wheel-and-tyre assembly rather than appearance.[^suspension-layout]

## Three angles that describe the wheel

The reference 2.9's geometry is easiest to understand by changing viewpoint. These terms describe what is measured; this chapter does not prescribe a universal road or track alignment.[^suspension-geometry]

**Camber** is the wheel's inclination from vertical, viewed from the front or rear. Negative camber puts the top inward towards the car's centre. It influences how the tyre presents its tread to the road as the body rolls and the suspension moves. Too much or too little for the conditions can compromise the working contact patch; static camber alone does not describe the wheel throughout a bend.

**Caster** is the fore-and-aft inclination of the front steering axis, viewed from the side. With positive caster, the axis's upper point lies rearward of its lower point. It contributes to steering self-centring and changes camber as the wheel steers. Caster is an axis angle, not the visible lean of a wheel viewed from the front.

**Toe** describes the directions the wheels point when viewed from above. Toe-in places their leading edges closer together; toe-out places them farther apart. Toe affects straight-line behaviour, steering response and tyre scrub. Rear toe is particularly relevant to the direction in which the rear axle guides the car. A rear wheel's small unintended change of direction can be felt as a change in the car's attitude.

Suspension travel changes geometry, and tyre forces can also deflect bushes. An alignment therefore measures a specified condition, rather than permanently fixing the wheel at one angle. Ride height, loading, tyre condition and the correct model specification matter when interpreting a printout. Changing springs or fitting adjustable aftermarket arms changes that context.

Uneven tread wear is evidence to investigate, not a complete diagnosis. Camber, toe, pressure, road use and worn components can all contribute. Correcting a number on an alignment screen cannot remove play from a joint or restore a damaged arm.

## Options on the 2010 2.9

The January 2010 UK catalogue lists **Porsche Active Suspension Management, code 475**, for both Boxster and S. On the ordinary models this combines electronically controlled damping with a ride height **10 mm lower** than the standard chassis. The lower static height belongs to the fitted suspension specification.[^suspension-options]

The driver selects **Normal** or **Sport**. Each is an operating range within which the controller varies damping at individual wheels according to vehicle movement and driving conditions. Sport generally requests firmer control, but can soften its damping response on rough surfaces. Normal can become firmer during more demanding driving. These are adaptive programmes rather than two permanently fixed damper settings.

The button changes hydraulic damping force. It does not change spring rate or select a different static ride height; the factory PASM installation uses a matched suspension specification. Actual static height also depends on the load carried.[^suspension-pasm]

Sport Chrono can request the Sport programme on a PASM-equipped car. PASM remains a separate option. The UK catalogue lists no separate 20 mm-lowered sports chassis for the ordinary Boxster or S.[^suspension-options]

The [mechanical differential option](../10-differential-driveshafts-and-mounts/index.mdx#options-on-the-2010-2.9) also brings suitably adapted rear anti-roll bars, according to Porsche’s technical introduction.[^suspension-front]

Changes in other 2.9 model years

The 2009 facelift retained the strut architecture while revising springs, dampers, anti-roll bars and tuning, including rear rebound stops and spring pads.[^suspension-rear] Those changes precede the reference car. Later US layout corroboration and unresolved annual UK details appear in the [annual matrix](../19-reference-atlas-and-index/index.mdx#annual-equipment-matrix).[^suspension-later]

<Heading level={2} id="s-spyder-and-black-edition">

The S, Spyder and Black Edition

</Heading>

The S shares the 2.9's basic suspension architecture and was also offered PASM in the 2010 UK catalogue. Spring, damper and bar specifications depend on the vehicle version, transmission and equipment.

The **Boxster Spyder** takes a different route. Its UK brochure specifies shorter, stiffer springs, anti-roll bars at both axles and dampers tuned for firm compression and rebound. The body sits **20 mm lower at both axles than the standard Boxster S**. This is a fixed sports suspension, distinct from the ordinary models' adaptive PASM option; it is not a third PASM button setting.[^suspension-spyder]

<Figure src={illustration8} alt={"A white 987 Boxster Spyder cornering in Porsche's period catalogue photograph, showing its 19-inch wheels and distinctive rear bodywork."}>

Spyder cornering on its fixed sports suspension. Photo: Porsche AG, *The Boxster Spyder* (UK, 01/10), p. 29. [Source](https://porschecarshistory.com/wp-content/old/brochures2/93/2010%20987%20Boxster%20Spyder.pdf).

</Figure>

The Spyder's lower vehicle mass and standard lightweight 19-inch wheels belong to that complete setup. A fixed lower chassis changes available clearance and suspension working position continuously; adaptive damping changes resistance during movement. They are different engineering choices even when both are described as sporty.

The **Boxster S Black Edition** has Spyder-design wheels with the ordinary chassis concept. Its March 2011 UK list retains PASM as an extra-cost option, with a 10 mm reduction and Normal/Sport modes.[^suspension-black]

<Heading level={2} id="identifying-and-assessing-the-reference-suspension">

Identification and ageing

</Heading>

Read the spring markings and damper identification. Porsche’s spring colour codes identify chassis version and spring tolerance together; they are more informative than the visible wheelarch gap, but are not a standalone model-year code.[^suspension-struts]

Ageing can affect several functions at once. Damper leakage or internal wear can reduce control; a damaged spring can change support; deteriorated mounts, bushes or joint boots can admit movement, noise or contamination. Corrosion, broken travel stops and non-matching replacement components deserve attention independently of mileage.

Establish the fitted configuration and investigate play or damage before interpreting an alignment or choosing replacement components.
