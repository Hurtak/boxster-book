# Suspension and wheel geometry

The standard **2010 Boxster 2.9** has independent suspension with coil-over McPherson struts at both ends. Coil springs carry the body, hydraulic dampers control its movement, aluminium arms and links guide the wheels, and an anti-roll bar links left and right at each axle. Electronically adjustable damping was an option, not part of the standard car.[^suspension-layout]

The job is harder than making a car feel firm. Each wheel must be free to follow a bump, yet keep pointing the right way and at the right angle while its tyre brakes, drives or corners. Meanwhile the occupants should feel much less movement than the wheel does. Geometry, springs, dampers and carefully chosen flexibility share that work.

## How a wheel is held

### At the front

The front wheel carrier holds the hub and its bearing. The strut rises from it to a mounting in the body. Below, a lateral lower control arm runs to the front crossmember through its inboard bush, and a separate guide runs forward from the arm to a body mounting. The steering tie rod adds one more constraint: moving it turns the carrier about its steering axis. Ball joints let these parts swivel while still transmitting force.

Porsche calls this a **strut suspension with a trailing link and wishbone**. The wishbone is the lower arm; the strut guides the wheel where a double-wishbone layout would use an upper arm.[^suspension-front]

The spring surrounds the damper, but they do different jobs, and the whole strut also carries structural loads between the carrier and its upper mounting. A thrust bearing under the front top mount lets the strut turn as the wheel steers, separately from the hub bearing on which the wheel spins. A worn mounting or bearing can affect noise isolation or steering feel.

No driveshaft reaches the front hub, which has a central retaining bolt and nut. A wheel-speed sensor on the carrier feeds the [braking and stability systems](../13-brakes-and-stability-systems/index.mdx).

### At the rear

The rear also uses McPherson-type struts, lower arms and fore-and-aft guides, with a separate link controlling toe, the direction in which the wheel points. With no steering rack, the struts and links must keep each wheel's direction under control as it moves and as tyre loads change. The driveshaft enters each rear hub, its joints letting the wheel move relative to the drivetrain.

Porsche's technical introduction calls this a McPherson rear axle, and its parts catalogue lists the rear wishbone, guide and track rod separately. The guide joins the lower arm; the toe-control rod joins the carrier at a separate point. They are three locating parts, not three names for the whole suspension. Equally, *toe-angle control* on a specification sheet does not mean powered rear-wheel steering.[^suspension-rear]

The rear suspension mounts on side pieces, a crossmember and bracing. Eccentric bolts at the lower-arm and toe-link mountings offset the pivots so that the geometry can be set; no actuator steers the rear wheels.

The [hub bearing](../10-differential-driveshafts-and-mounts/index.mdx#driveshaft-joints-plunge-and-the-hub) lets the wheel spin; the ball joints let the guiding links change angle.

## Springs and dampers

On the standard 2.9, each coil spring is compressed even at rest. Acting through the suspension geometry, it is the main support for the body at that corner, and luggage or a passenger changes that balance. Over a bump the wheel rises relative to the body, compressing the spring further and storing energy, which the spring returns as it extends.

**Spring rate** is the extra force needed for each extra amount of compression. It is not the spring's free length, and neither figure alone sets the ride height: installed geometry, spring seats, load and the rest of the spring specification all matter. The rate felt at the tyre also depends on how far the spring moves for a given wheel movement.

Without enough damping, the spring's stored energy would keep passing between body and wheel, and the car would go on bouncing. The hydraulic damper resists movement by forcing oil through controlled passages and valves, turning that energy into heat. **Compression**, or bump, shortens the damper and **rebound** extends it. The resistance can differ in each direction.[^suspension-damping]

Damper force depends on movement, particularly piston velocity: how fast the damper changes length, not how fast the car is going. Crawling over a sharp edge can move the damper quickly, while a fast car on a gentle undulation may move it slowly. That is why “high-speed damping” refers to fast damper movement, not to fast driving.

Porsche describes the standard struts as coil springs with double-tube gas-filled dampers. The gas charge helps the damper work consistently; the metal coil spring still carries the car. The parts drawings show the spring seats and isolators separately from the bellows, which protects the damper rod, and the compressible auxiliary stop, which helps manage the load near the end of compression travel.[^suspension-struts]

## Anti-roll bars

Each anti-roll bar is a torsion spring linking the left and right suspension. Drop links join its arms to the struts, and bushes in brackets hold it to the axle structure while letting its centre section rotate. If both wheels rise equally, in an ideal symmetrical layout, the bar largely rotates without extra twist; the road springs still resist that movement.

When one wheel rises relative to the other, the bar's arms move differently and its centre section twists, resisting the difference. In a bend, with the outside suspension compressing and the inside extending, that adds roll stiffness. Over a one-wheel bump it also passes some of the disturbance to the other side.

Adding roll stiffness is therefore a compromise, not free grip. Its split between front and rear affects how cornering load transfer is shared between the axles, and the tyres, springs, geometry and road surface all play a part. A body that corners flatter does not by itself prove more grip.

## Compliance and unsprung mass

Rubber bushes give selected joints a controlled flexibility and reduce the vibration reaching the body. Their stiffness helps decide how far each joint moves under load.

Under braking or cornering, those small elastic movements can shift the wheel's position and toe. This is **compliance**, distinct from the geometry change of ordinary suspension travel. A split, separated or overly mobile bush can let the tyre steer the wheel more than intended, and the car may feel unsettled even when a stationary alignment reading looks plausible.

The body, passengers and powertrain are mainly **sprung mass**. Wheels, tyres, hubs, brakes and wheel carriers contribute to **unsprung mass**, which follows the road more directly, and so do parts of the arms and driveshafts; the boundary is not simply everything below the spring. Less mass following a sharp bump can make the wheel easier to control, though the outcome still depends on the tyres and damping.

Porsche uses aluminium extensively in the moving suspension parts to help keep their mass down without making the wheel-guiding structure unduly flexible. A larger wheel is not automatically lighter. Judge any claimed saving on the complete fitted wheel and tyre, not on appearance.[^suspension-layout]

## Camber, caster and toe

The 2.9's geometry is easiest to grasp by viewing the wheel from three directions. These terms describe what is measured; this chapter does not prescribe a road or track alignment.[^suspension-geometry]

**Camber** is the wheel's lean from vertical, seen from the front or rear. Negative camber tips the top inwards, towards the centre of the car. Camber affects how the tread meets the road as the body rolls and the suspension moves. Too much or too little for the conditions can compromise the working contact patch, and the static figure does not describe the wheel throughout a bend.

**Caster** is the fore-and-aft tilt of the front steering axis, seen from the side. With positive caster, the top of the axis sits behind the bottom. Caster helps the steering self-centre and changes camber as the wheel steers. It is an axis angle, not a lean of the wheel visible from the front.

**Toe** is where the wheels point, seen from above. Toe-in brings their leading edges closer together; toe-out spreads them apart. Toe affects straight-line behaviour, steering response and tyre scrub. Rear toe matters particularly because it affects the direction in which the rear axle guides the car, and even a small unintended change can be felt as a change in the car's attitude.

<Figure src={illustration7} alt={"Camber is shown from the front, caster from the side and toe from above. Negative camber leans the tops inward; positive caster places the steering-axis top rearward; toe-in brings the wheel fronts closer together."}>

Camber from the front, caster from the side and toe from above. Negative camber leans the tops of the wheels inwards, positive caster tilts the top of the steering axis rearwards, and toe-in brings the fronts of the wheels closer together. The angles are exaggerated, and none is an alignment target for the Boxster.

</Figure>

Suspension travel changes the geometry and tyre forces flex the bushes, so an alignment measures one specified condition rather than fixing the wheel at those angles. Ride height, load, tyre condition and the correct model specification all matter when reading a printout, and different springs or adjustable aftermarket arms change that context.

Uneven tread wear is a clue, not a diagnosis: camber, toe, pressure, road use and worn parts can all contribute. Correcting a number on an alignment screen cannot remove play from a joint or repair a damaged arm.

## Options on the 2010 2.9

The January 2010 UK catalogue lists **Porsche Active Suspension Management, code 475**, for both Boxster and S. On the ordinary models it combines electronically controlled damping with a ride height **10 mm lower** than the standard chassis.[^suspension-options]

The driver selects **Normal** or **Sport**. Neither is a fixed setting: each is a range within which the controller varies damping at individual wheels according to the car's movement and the driving conditions. Sport generally asks for firmer control but can soften on rough surfaces, while Normal can firm up in harder driving.

The button changes hydraulic damping force, not spring rate or static ride height. The lower height comes from the factory PASM car's own matched suspension, and still depends on load.[^suspension-pasm]

Sport Chrono can call up the Sport programme on a PASM car, but PASM remains a separate option. The UK catalogue lists no 20 mm-lowered sports chassis for the ordinary Boxster or S.[^suspension-options]

Porsche's technical introduction adds that the [mechanical differential option](../10-differential-driveshafts-and-mounts/index.mdx#options-on-the-2010-2.9) brings suitably adapted rear anti-roll bars.[^suspension-front]

<Heading level={2} id="other-2.9-model-years">

Changes in other 2.9 model years

</Heading>

The 2009 facelift, which preceded the reference car, kept the strut layout but revised the springs, dampers, anti-roll bars and tuning, including the rear rebound stops and spring pads.[^suspension-rear] Later US corroboration of the layout, and the UK annual details still unresolved, are in the [annual matrix](../19-reference-atlas-and-index/index.mdx#annual-equipment-matrix).[^suspension-later]

<Heading level={2} id="s-spyder-and-black-edition">

The S, Spyder and Black Edition

</Heading>

The S shares the 2.9's basic layout and was also offered PASM in the 2010 UK catalogue. Spring, damper and anti-roll-bar specifications depend on the model, transmission and equipment.

The **Boxster Spyder** is different. Its UK brochure specifies shorter, stiffer springs, anti-roll bars at both axles and dampers tuned for firm compression and rebound, with the body **20 mm lower at both axles than the standard Boxster S**. This fixed sports suspension is neither the ordinary models' adaptive PASM option nor a third PASM setting.[^suspension-spyder]

<Figure src={illustration8} alt={"A white 987 Boxster Spyder cornering in Porsche's period catalogue photograph, showing its 19-inch wheels and distinctive rear bodywork."}>

Boxster Spyder cornering on its fixed sports suspension. Photo: Porsche AG, *The Boxster Spyder* (UK, 01/10), p. 29. [Source](https://porschecarshistory.com/wp-content/old/brochures2/93/2010%20987%20Boxster%20Spyder.pdf).

</Figure>

The Spyder's lower weight and standard lightweight 19-inch wheels belong to the same package. The Spyder's fixed chassis sits 20 mm lower than the standard S, permanently changing the clearance and the suspension's working position. PASM's adaptive damping varies the resistance during movement; its own 10 mm reduction is a fixed part of the PASM car's suspension, not something the button changes.

The **Boxster S Black Edition** combines Spyder-design wheels with the ordinary chassis. Its March 2011 UK price list keeps PASM as an extra-cost option, with a 10 mm reduction and Normal and Sport modes.[^suspension-black]

<Heading level={2} id="identifying-and-assessing-the-reference-suspension">

Identification and ageing

</Heading>

Read the spring markings and damper identification. Porsche's spring colour codes identify chassis version and spring tolerance together. They say more than the wheelarch gap, but are not a model-year code on their own.[^suspension-struts]

Ageing can affect several functions at once. Damper leakage or internal wear can reduce control; a damaged spring changes the support; deteriorated mounts, bushes or joint boots admit movement, noise or dirt. Corrosion, broken travel stops and mismatched replacement parts deserve attention whatever the mileage.

Establish what is fitted, and check for play or damage, before interpreting an alignment or choosing replacement parts.
