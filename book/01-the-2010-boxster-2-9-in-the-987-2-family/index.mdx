# The 2010 Boxster 2.9 in the 987.2 family

This book is about one car: the 2010 Porsche Boxster with the 2.9-litre engine, as sold in Britain and continental Europe. It is a two-seat roadster with a powered fabric roof, a flat-six engine mounted behind the seats, and rear-wheel drive. The standard gearbox is a six-speed manual. Porsche's seven-speed Doppelkupplung, or PDK, was an option, and the book covers both.[^baseline]

Every chapter begins with that car in its standard form. Options come next, then the changes Porsche made in other years, and finally the bigger-engined relatives. Taking one fixed car as the reference keeps the comparisons honest: when the text says the S is more powerful, or the Spyder lighter, it means compared with this car.

## What 987.2 means

**987** is Porsche's internal type number for the second-generation Boxster. The generation came in two phases. Porsche Classic lists the first as 2005–2008 and the revised cars as 2009–2012. This book calls them **987.1** and **987.2**. The suffixes are shorthand, not names Porsche printed in its brochures. The type number on its own does not tell you which engine or gearbox a car has.[^generations]

The revision was much more than a styling refresh. It brought new engines, made a six-speed manual standard and replaced the Tiptronic S automatic with PDK. Chassis, steering, stability control and styling were all revised too. [The yearly timeline](../02-the-yearly-timeline/index.mdx) dates their introduction.[^facelift]

Its successor, the **981**, arrived during 2012 and is a different car underneath. It has a new chassis, a longer wheelbase, electromechanical steering in place of hydraulic, and a 2.7-litre direct-injection base engine. Check the generation, not just the registration year, before relying on a manual, a brochure or a parts listing.[^981]

## A roadster arranged around its engine

Two terms describe the layout, and they answer different questions. **Mid-engine** says where the engine sits in the car: between the axles, in this case behind the occupants and ahead of the rear wheels. **Flat-six** describes the engine itself: two banks of three cylinders lying horizontally on either side of the crankshaft.[^layout]

That layout sets the order of spaces along the car. There is a luggage compartment at the front, then the two seats, then the engine and the well where the roof folds, then a second luggage compartment at the back. The roof has its own stowage space, so both boots stay usable with the top down.[^luggage]

The order of spaces from front to back, with the car facing left. The drawing shows where the engine and the two boots sit; the shapes and spacing are schematic.

The rear boot gives access to the oil and coolant filler points, but not to the engine itself, which sits under separate covers, explained in [body structure and packaging](../04-body-structure-and-packaging/index.mdx#three-different-spaces-behind-the-seats).[^luggage]

Placing the heavy engine near the middle of the car affects how it turns. A long pole is easier to spin when you hold it by the middle and the weight is near your hands than when heavy loads hang from its ends. A car's equivalent motion is *yaw*, its rotation seen from above as it changes direction. Keeping mass near the centre generally makes yaw easier to start and stop. How the car actually behaves still depends on the tyres, the suspension, the road and the driver.

The engine and gearbox are bolted together into one drive unit. The gearbox casing also contains the final drive and the differential, a combination called a **transaxle**. Two driveshafts run from it to the rear hubs. The front wheels steer, while the rear tyres both guide the car and put the power down. The mechanical chapters follow that path from the crankshaft to the road.

## The ordinary 2.9 and its relatives

Porsche's catalogue calls the ordinary car simply **Boxster**. This book adds "2.9" where it helps to be clear. The **Boxster S** is a separate model with a larger engine, not an option package or a gearbox choice. Both were offered with the manual or PDK.[^baseline]

The two engines feed fuel differently. The 2.9 sprays fuel into each inlet port, upstream of the valves. The S has a 3.4-litre engine with direct fuel injection, **DFI**, which sprays fuel straight into the cylinders. Both engines arrived with the revised generation.[^injection]

*Pick a relative to compare its engine and purpose with the 2.9's 188 kW / 255 PS. The bars show published peak power only. They say nothing about acceleration, handling or value.*

Two later 3.4-litre models complete the family. The **Boxster Spyder** is a lightweight production model with a higher-output 3.4-litre DFI engine. Its roof, body and chassis were all designed around saving weight.[^spyder] The **Boxster S Black Edition** has the same peak power but a different aim. It is a well-equipped S in a black colour scheme, keeps the powered fabric roof, and was limited to 987 cars.[^black] Where these cars differ from the 2.9 in a way that matters for a particular system, that chapter says so.

## Where the Cayman fits

The **Cayman** is the Boxster's fixed-roof coupé sibling. Porsche often documented the two cars together, which makes shared technical material useful. Their specifications still differ, and so can complete assemblies.[^cayman] The [engine chapter](../05-engine-construction-and-valve-gear/index.mdx#the-s-spyder-black-edition-and-cayman) gives an example. Whenever a Cayman photograph is used to show a shared part, the caption says so.

## How to read this book

**Units.** Power is given in kW and PS. Porsche's English-language brochures of the period label their metric figures "hp". This book writes those figures as PS, because they are not British bhp. Performance and weight figures are always tied to the gearbox, equipment and measuring standard they were quoted for.

**Dates.** An announcement date, a build date, a model year and a first-registration date are four different things. A car registered later is not necessarily built to a later specification. Equipment availability comes from dated UK publications. North American documents are identified as such wherever they are used. Later parts catalogues record service applications and replacement parts, so a shared listing alone does not prove that the original parts were identical, or that whole assemblies swap across.

**Individual cars.** A build record shows how a car left the factory. Inspection shows what is fitted now, and service invoices explain any difference. A symptom describes what the car does; it does not prove which part has failed. Note when it happens, under what conditions, and what can be measured before choosing a repair. [Reading an individual car's record](../03-the-2010-boxster-2-9-specification-and-options/index.mdx#reading-an-individual-cars-record) explains the process in more detail.

**Illustrations.** The original diagrams simplify shapes, routes and movements so that the relationships stay clear. Treat them as schematics unless a dimension is cited. The interactive figures let you change one thing at a time; they do not reproduce Porsche's control software. Porsche's numbered parts drawings show the real hardware. Their exploded spacing is there for legibility, so it shows neither working clearances nor the order of removal. Photograph captions name the variant pictured and where the image came from.

**Route through the book.** Chapters 2 and 3 set out the dates and the equipment. Chapters 4 to 10 follow the body and the powertrain, from engine to driven wheels. Chapters 11 to 13 cover how the car meets the road: suspension, steering, tyres and brakes. Chapters 14 to 17 cover the roof, cabin, electrics and entertainment. Chapter 18 brings the systems together around occupant protection. The [reference appendix](../19-reference-atlas-and-index/index.mdx) follows, with the year-by-year equipment matrix, picture atlases, option codes and a glossary. In the matrix, a documented carryover is marked differently from an unverified entry.
